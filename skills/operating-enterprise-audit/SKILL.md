# Operating Enterprise Audit

## Purpose

Real estate underwriting overwhelmingly treats buildings as passive financial instruments. Drucker's discipline is that every asset is an operating enterprise whose results depend on management, customers, and the match between mission and execution. This skill forces that lens onto every deal.

## Protocol

### Step 1: State the Mission

In one sentence, state what business this asset is in. Not "a Class A office building in Midtown" — that is a description, not a mission. A mission is: "Providing secure, prestige headquarters environments for financial-services tenants that need to recruit senior talent in a face-to-face industry." If you cannot write a mission sentence with a named customer type and named value delivered, the deal has no operating identity.

### Step 2: Identify the Customer

Name the customer precisely. Who decides to rent here? Who influences that decision? Who pays? Who uses the space day to day? These are often four different people. Drucker's question — "Who is the customer?" — has multiple answers and they all matter.

### Step 3: Identify What the Customer Considers Value

For each named customer, state what they actually value. Not what you think they should value. Not what the brokerage deck claims. Ask: if this customer left tomorrow, what would the exit interview say? If nobody on the team can answer that, the deal does not know its customer.

### Step 4: Define the Results

State, in measurable terms, what results the enterprise must produce:

- **Retention rate** (tenants renewing at market rent)
- **Occupancy** (physical and economic)
- **Net operating income growth** (organic, not from rent bumps alone)
- **Customer satisfaction** (tenant survey scores, complaint volume)
- **Employee performance** (the property management team's metrics)

If any of these is absent from the business plan, the plan has no steering mechanism.

### Step 5: Audit the Management System

Ask the five operating questions:

1. What are the property manager and asset manager accountable for, measured in numbers, reviewed how often?
2. What decisions can they make without escalation? What must they escalate?
3. What training and development is planned for the on-site team?
4. What is the plan to replace the property manager if they leave tomorrow?
5. How does the operator learn from other assets in the portfolio? What is the feedback loop?

A deal whose operating system cannot answer these questions is a deal whose results are random.

### Step 6: Apply the Abandonment Test

Drucker's most famous question: if we were not already in this business, would we enter it today on these terms? Answer for the asset, not the developer's ego. If the answer is no, the only honest options are exit, reposition, or explicit justification for continuing.

### Step 7: Write the Enterprise Verdict

> "Mission: [one sentence]. Customer: [named]. Customer value: [what they actually buy]. Required results: [metrics]. Management system: [strong / weak, with reasons]. Abandonment test: [pass / fail]. [Proceed / reposition / abandon]."

## When to Apply

Every deal involving ongoing operations — which is every deal except raw land. Especially critical for asset classes with intensive operations (hotels, storage, multifamily, medical office, senior living) and for portfolio acquisitions where management transition is the biggest hidden risk.

## When Not to Apply

Pure land banking where no operating enterprise exists. Even then, note that an operating enterprise will eventually have to exist for the asset to pay out, and the plan should name who will run it.

## Integration With Other Skills

This skill addresses organizational and operational survivability. Combine with Buffett's replacement cost test (pricing floor), Dalio's four-quadrant test (macro survivability), and Zell's supply pipeline check (market survivability).
