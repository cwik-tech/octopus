# Partnership And Civic Leverage Audit

## Purpose

Max Fisher's career teaches that the best real estate returns come not from finding the best deal but from assembling the best group to execute the deal — and from embedding that group in the civic infrastructure of the place where the deal lives. The Irvine Ranch acquisition (Fisher, Taubman, and Henry Ford II turning $337 million into $1 billion in six years) succeeded because each partner brought a capability the others lacked, and because the group understood the political and civic landscape of Orange County. This skill forces the crew to audit both dimensions before capital moves.

## Protocol

### Step 1: Define the Capability Requirements

List the specific capabilities this deal requires to succeed. Common seats at the table:

- **Capital partner** — provides the equity or arranges the debt
- **Operating partner** — manages the asset day to day
- **Development partner** — if there is construction or repositioning, who runs the build?
- **Local market expert** — deep submarket knowledge, broker relationships, tenant pipeline
- **Political and civic navigator** — relationships with zoning boards, planning commissions, city councils, community groups
- **Exit partner** — relationships with the buyer universe that will matter at disposition
- **Legal and structuring counsel** — complex deal structures, tax, partnership governance

Not every deal needs all seven seats. Name the ones this deal needs and justify removing any you omit.

### Step 2: Name the Partners

For each required seat, name a specific person or firm. For each:

- What they bring (in one sentence)
- What they have done in this asset class, submarket, or deal type before
- How well you know them — direct repeated experience, single prior deal, reputation only, or stranger
- What they need from the deal (financial return, operating fees, civic visibility, portfolio diversification)

If any seat is empty, the deal is not ready. The partner search is the next task, not the underwriting.

### Step 3: Grade the Partnership Chemistry

Partnerships fail under stress. For the assembled group, answer:

- Have these specific people worked together before? On what? How did it go?
- If they have not worked together, what is the evidence that their styles are compatible? (Fisher and Taubman succeeded because their skills were complementary and their styles, while different, were tested over decades.)
- What is the most likely source of disagreement among the partners — and has it been discussed?
- Is there a clear lead partner with decision authority, or is authority diffuse? Diffuse authority is the leading cause of partnership paralysis.

Score the chemistry: **proven** (repeated successful collaboration), **plausible** (compatible skills and styles, untested together), or **risky** (no history, no evidence of compatibility, or known friction).

### Step 4: Map the Civic and Political Landscape

For the jurisdiction where the deal sits, list the key civic and political actors:

- **Elected officials** with jurisdiction (mayor, city council members, county commissioners)
- **Appointed officials** with authority (planning director, zoning administrator, building department head)
- **Community organizations** with influence (neighborhood associations, business improvement districts, civic leagues, religious institutions)
- **Media voices** who shape public perception of development in this area
- **Institutional anchors** whose support or opposition will matter (hospital systems, universities, major employers)

For each, state:

- Their current posture toward development generally and toward this kind of project specifically
- Whether anyone on the partnership table has a direct, personal relationship with them
- The last time someone on the team was in the room with them

### Step 5: Assess the Civic Leverage Depth

Grade the civic footing on a three-level scale:

- **Deep** — the partnership has direct, personal, multi-year relationships with the key civic actors. There is a history of engagement, contributions, and trust. The team knows the civic actors' priorities and can navigate the approval process with confidence. (Fisher in Detroit; Fisher and Taubman in Michigan politics.)
- **Developing** — the partnership has some relationships, is actively building others, and has a credible plan for the rest. Approval timelines will be longer and less predictable.
- **Absent** — the partnership has no civic presence in this jurisdiction. Any approval process is a coin flip, and any opposition is unmanageable. This is a red flag unless the deal requires no discretionary approvals.

If civic leverage is absent and the deal requires discretionary approvals (rezoning, special permits, public-private partnership, tax incentives), the deal should not proceed until civic relationships are built or a civic partner is added to the table.

### Step 6: Audit the Partnership Structure

Review the legal and governance structure:

- **Decision authority** — who makes day-to-day decisions? Who makes capital calls? Who approves an exit?
- **Economic alignment** — are the partners' incentives aligned, or does someone benefit from a different outcome than the others?
- **Exit provisions** — can one partner force a sale? Can one partner block a sale? What happens if a partner dies, divorces, or goes bankrupt?
- **Dispute resolution** — is there a named mechanism (mediation, arbitration, buy-sell) before litigation?
- **Reporting and transparency** — how often does the group meet, and what information flows to all partners?

Fisher's career lesson: the deal that destroys wealth is usually the deal where the partnership structure was not tested before it was stressed.

### Step 7: Run the Relationship Capital ROI Test

Fisher did not deploy his best relationships on small deals. Ask:

- Is this opportunity large enough to justify the relationship capital being spent?
- If this deal succeeds, does it strengthen the partnership for the next deal, or is it a one-off?
- If this deal fails, does it damage relationships that are worth more than the capital at risk?

The test is not just financial return on investment (ROI). It is relationship ROI — whether the act of doing this deal together makes the partners more capable and more connected for the deals that follow.

### Step 8: Write the Partnership and Civic Verdict

> "Capability seats required: [list]. Partners named: [list with roles]. Empty seats: [list or none]. Partnership chemistry: [proven / plausible / risky]. Civic leverage: [deep / developing / absent — with key actors named]. Partnership structure: [clear / needs work / absent]. Relationship capital ROI: [justified / marginal / unjustified]. [Proceed with this group / find the missing partner first / build civic footing first / pass]."

## When to Apply

Every deal that involves more than one equity party, any deal requiring discretionary government approvals, any deal in a jurisdiction where the investor has not previously operated, and any deal large enough that its failure would damage the firm's reputation and relationships.

## When Not to Apply

Small, all-cash, as-of-right acquisitions in a market where the investor has deep existing presence and no partners. Even then, note that the civic landscape should be checked annually — it shifts with elections and appointments.
