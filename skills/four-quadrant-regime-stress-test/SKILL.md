# Four-Quadrant Regime Stress Test

## Purpose

Real estate is a levered bet on interest rates, credit availability, and inflation — whether operators admit it or not. Most deal underwriting assumes the current macro environment will persist throughout the hold. This skill forces the crew to explicitly stress every deal across the four possible macro regimes and to confront where the position would actually break.

## The Four Quadrants

| Quadrant | Growth | Inflation | Typical conditions |
|----------|--------|-----------|--------------------|
| Q1 | Rising | Rising | Boom, late-cycle expansion, hot economy |
| Q2 | Rising | Falling | Goldilocks, productivity growth, low-rate expansion |
| Q3 | Falling | Rising | Stagflation, supply shock, policy trap |
| Q4 | Falling | Falling | Recession, deleveraging, deflation risk |

## Protocol

### Step 1: Define the Base Case

State the deal's assumed rent growth, vacancy, cap rate at exit, and debt cost at each refinance. This is your baseline.

### Step 2: Map Each Quadrant to Variable Movements

For each of the four quadrants, state how the following move relative to the base case. Use specific numbers, not adjectives.

- **Rent growth** (annual % versus base case)
- **Vacancy** (percentage points versus base case)
- **Cap rate at exit** (basis points versus base case)
- **Debt cost at refinance** (basis points versus base case)
- **Construction costs** (affects replacement cost and new supply economics)

### Step 3: Re-Run the Underwriting in Each Quadrant

In each quadrant, compute:

- **IRR to equity** at the hold period
- **DSCR** (Debt Service Coverage Ratio) at the most stressed year
- **LTV** (Loan-to-Value) at the refinance date
- **Time to recovery** if the quadrant produces a loss year

### Step 4: Classify Each Quadrant's Outcome

| Category | Definition |
|----------|------------|
| Accretive | Hits or exceeds base-case returns |
| Neutral | Returns under base case but positive, no covenant breaches |
| Stressed | Losses or covenant breaches, but survivable without fresh capital |
| Catastrophic | Requires fresh capital, forced sale, or default |

### Step 5: Apply the Survival Rule

A deal must be **non-catastrophic in at least three of the four quadrants**. A deal that is catastrophic in more than one quadrant must clear one of these higher bars before proceeding:

1. The investor is positioned to benefit from the failing quadrants elsewhere in the portfolio (explicit hedge).
2. The deal is small enough that catastrophic loss is absorbable.
3. There is a specific, written thesis for why the failing quadrants are structurally unlikely — not just currently unlikely.

### Step 6: Map the Debt Schedule Against the Quadrants

For every debt maturity during the hold period, state what rate environment each quadrant implies and whether refinancing clears under that environment. Debt maturity in a stagflation scenario is the single most common failure mode in real estate.

### Step 7: Write the Regime Verdict

Summarize in one paragraph:

> "Base case IRR is [X]. Under Q1 (growth↑ inflation↑), outcome is [category]. Under Q2, [category]. Under Q3, [category]. Under Q4, [category]. The deal is [survivable / not survivable] under the four-quadrant rule. The most dangerous regime is [Q], and the mechanism is [rate spike at refinance / rent collapse / cap rate blowout]."

## When to Apply

Every real estate deal with leverage, with a hold period longer than 24 months, or with a refinancing assumption. In practice, that is every deal.

## When Not to Apply

Very small, all-cash, short-hold positions where the macro regime cannot meaningfully affect the outcome. These are rare.

## Integration With Other Skills

This skill defines *macro survivability*. Combine with Buffett's replacement-cost stress test (asset-level survivability), Zell's supply pipeline gut check (sector-level survivability), and Taleb's ruin scenario mapping (tail-event survivability).
