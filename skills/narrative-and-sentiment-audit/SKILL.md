# Narrative And Sentiment Audit

## Purpose

Barbara Corcoran built The Corcoran Group on the insight that residential markets move on sentiment and story, not on spreadsheets. The Corcoran Report made the firm famous by publishing a narrative about the market before the data did. This skill forces the crew to treat narrative and sentiment as first-order economic variables rather than soft inputs, and to commit real budget to presentation and positioning.

## Protocol

### Step 1: Write the Story of the Place

In one paragraph, write the story of this place as a buyer or renter would encounter it. Not the features — the story. What is the character of the neighborhood? What kind of person lives here and what does their weekend look like? What is the before-and-after arc of the area over the last 10 years, and what is the next 10? What is the place becoming?

Then name the buyer. Not a demographic bucket — a person. "A 34-year-old creative-director couple who just had their first kid and are leaving the West Village because the school options ran out." That is a buyer. "Urban professionals, 30-45" is not.

If you cannot write the story and name the buyer, you cannot price the asset.

### Step 2: Read the Ground-Level Sentiment

The data follows the sentiment by 4–12 weeks. Gather the sentiment directly:

- **Showings per week** — at comparable listings, not just yours
- **Open-house traffic** — and the quality of attendance (browsers vs. pre-qualified buyers)
- **Weekend energy** — are the streets busy? Are the restaurants full? Is there line-out-the-door energy at the new cafe?
- **Broker chatter** — what are 5 active agents in the submarket actually saying when you ask them off the record?
- **Social signals** — is the neighborhood being posted about, and by whom?

Write a one-paragraph sentiment read and compare it to the comps. If sentiment and comps diverge, the sentiment usually wins on a 90-day horizon.

### Step 3: Identify the Branding Opportunity

Most residential assets are sold with names and positioning that were either inherited, generic, or invented in a meeting with no customer in mind. Ask:

- Is there a name for this place that does not yet exist and would stick if we used it first? (The Corcoran Group's greatest hits include giving neighborhoods names that the market then adopted.)
- Is there a narrative frame that repositions the asset away from a weak comp set and toward a stronger one?
- Is there a signature detail — an architectural element, a story about the building, a connection to something culturally legible — that becomes the hook?

Write the one-sentence positioning line. Test it against the named buyer.

### Step 4: Draft the One-Paragraph Press Release

Write a one-paragraph press release about this asset as if it were being announced tomorrow. Then ask, honestly: would a real journalist at a real publication cover this? If yes, which one and why? If no, there is no earned-media opportunity, and the marketing plan is going to be pure paid — which is expensive, commodity, and fragile.

### Step 5: Audit the Presentation Plan

Presentation is not a discretionary cost; it is the product in residential real estate. State the specific budget commitments:

- **Photography** — who, how much, including twilight and drone where relevant
- **Staging** — scope, style, cost, and the specific reason each room is staged the way it is
- **Video and virtual tour** — included or cut
- **Marketing collateral** — brochure quality, print or digital, distribution
- **Digital advertising** — channels and budget
- **Events and open houses** — cadence, catering where relevant, broker outreach

If the total presentation budget is under 0.5% of the asking price, it is underinvestment and should be justified.

### Step 6: Evaluate the Listing Voice

The listing agent is the deal's public voice. Ask:

- When they describe the property, do they lead with confidence or with hedges?
- Do they have a story to tell, or a feature list to recite?
- Have they sold in this submarket before, and do they have the buyer list that matches the named buyer from Step 1?
- When a tough question is asked (price, comps, flaws), do they answer directly or deflect?

If the agent is a hedger on a sale you actually want to win, replace them or coach them. Hedging agents leave money on the table every time.

### Step 7: Map the Media Relationships

List the media relationships that could carry the story:

- Local real estate reporters and columnists
- Lifestyle and design publications that cover this kind of asset
- Neighborhood newsletters and blogs with real audience
- Broker network amplification (who shares with whom)

State who on the team has each relationship. If the answer is nobody, the deal's media plan is a hope, not a plan.

### Step 8: Write the Narrative Verdict

> "Story of the place: [paragraph]. Named buyer: [specific person]. Sentiment read: [summary and divergence from comps]. Positioning line: [one sentence]. Press release test: [pass/fail, and which journalist]. Presentation budget: [total and key line items]. Listing voice: [confident / hedging — and the plan]. Media relationships: [named or absent]. [Buy and market hard / reposition the brand / pass unless the story changes]."

## When to Apply

Every residential deal, mixed-use deal with a residential component, and any acquisition where the value creation story depends on shifting perception of a place or a building.

## When Not to Apply

Pure operating trades in institutional asset classes where the buyer is another institution and the sale is a spreadsheet transaction (core office to a pension fund, for example). Even then, note that if the asset will ever touch a consumer — tenants, visitors, public — the narrative still matters at the operating level.
