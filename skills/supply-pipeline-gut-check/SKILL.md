# Supply Pipeline Gut Check

## Purpose

Real estate is, over any meaningful horizon, a supply-demand business at the submarket level. Brokers sell demand stories. Zell's discipline is to force the supply side into the conversation with real counts, then let the math determine whether the deal deserves to exist. This skill produces that forcing function.

## Protocol

### Step 1: Define the Submarket Precisely

State the submarket in one sentence with real geographic boundaries — not the metro, not the county, not the MSA unless the MSA truly behaves as one market. For most asset classes the relevant radius is 3–5 miles or a named district. If you cannot draw it on a map, you cannot underwrite it.

### Step 2: Count the Pipeline

Gather three counts for the submarket, by asset class:

- **Under construction** — units actively being built, with delivery dates
- **Permitted, not yet started** — units with active permits, no shovel
- **Proposed** — units in entitlement or announced but unpermitted

Use CoStar, local planning department records, and direct developer conversations. Do not trust a single source. A permitted project is real until it dies; a proposed project is a rumor until it permits.

### Step 3: Build the Delivery Schedule

Plot deliveries by quarter over the next 36 months. Apply these haircuts to the raw counts:

- Under construction: 95% deliver (slippage is weeks, not cancellation)
- Permitted not started: 60% deliver within the window
- Proposed: 25% deliver within the window

These are starting haircuts. Adjust upward in capital-abundant regimes, downward when construction financing is tight.

### Step 4: Compute Forward Absorption Required

State current submarket absorption (net units leased or sold per quarter over the trailing 4 quarters). Then compute:

`months of supply = (projected deliveries over next 12 months) / (trailing annual absorption / 12)`

If the result is greater than 12 months, the submarket is structurally oversupplied on the 12-month horizon. Above 18 months, walk unless the deal has a specific story for why it wins the absorption race.

### Step 5: Identify the Marginal Competitor

Name the single project most likely to pull tenants or buyers away from yours. Know the rents, the concessions, the amenities, the delivery date, and the developer's balance sheet. If you cannot name this competitor, you have not studied the submarket.

### Step 6: Stress the Pipeline

Ask the inverse question: what would the pipeline have to look like for this deal to fail? If even a modestly worse pipeline would break the thesis, the margin is too thin.

### Step 7: Write the Gut Check Verdict

> "Submarket pipeline over 36 months is [X] under construction, [Y] permitted, [Z] proposed. Haircut-adjusted deliveries next 12 months: [N]. Trailing absorption: [A]. Months of supply: [M]. Most dangerous competitor: [project, rent, delivery]. [Proceed / walk / negotiate harder]."

## When to Apply

Every deal. Zell's most-quoted principle is that he makes his money on the way in. The pipeline check is how you verify you are actually making it on the way in.

## When Not to Apply

Only when the asset is so small and idiosyncratic that submarket supply is irrelevant (a single-tenant mission-critical lease with a 20-year term to an investment-grade tenant, for example). These are rare and should be called out explicitly when claimed.

## Integration With Other Skills

This skill handles sector and submarket survivability. Combine with Buffett's replacement cost test (if new supply is unprofitable at replacement, the pipeline will thin naturally), Dalio's four-quadrant regime test (capital-tight regimes kill proposed projects faster), and Gray's thematic gut check (national flows shape local pipelines with a lag).
