# Human Dynamics Deal Review

## Purpose

Iger's career lesson is that every major deal is a human transaction dressed in financial clothing. The Pixar, Marvel, and Lucasfilm deals all hinged on a small number of individuals — their trust, their ambitions, and the relationship he personally built with them. Real estate is the same: the sponsor's character, the tenant's key decision-maker, the city official with pocket veto, the legacy owner deciding whether to sell to you. This skill forces those human variables to the front of the memo, where they belong.

## Protocol

### Step 1: Map the Principal Actors

List every human whose decision can materially change the deal outcome. Not just the signatories — the actual decision-makers. For each, note:

- Name and role
- Formal authority (what they can decide on their own)
- Informal authority (what they can block, delay, or accelerate)
- Reporting line (who they answer to and who influences them)

If this list exceeds 10 people, narrow it to the critical 5-7. If it is under 3, you are missing someone.

### Step 2: Assess Trust With Each Actor

For each principal, rate the current trust level:

- **High trust** — direct relationship, repeated successful interactions, they would take your call
- **Working trust** — professional relationship, no history of friction, would return your call within a day
- **Thin trust** — know of each other, no real history, transactional only
- **No trust** — no relationship, or worse, a negative one
- **Broken trust** — specific prior incident that must be addressed before anything else

Deals with any "broken" or more than one "no trust" entry on critical actors require explicit relationship repair before diligence dollars are spent.

### Step 3: Read the Character Signals

For the sponsor, seller, and any GP you are backing, collect specific evidence of character:

- How do they treat people who can do nothing for them?
- How have they behaved in prior downturns? (This is the single highest-signal question. Past-downturn behavior predicts next-downturn behavior.)
- What is the reputation they have with former employees and former partners?
- Do their stated motivations match their observed behavior over time?
- When something went wrong in the past, did they own it or did they blame the environment?

Write a one-paragraph character read based on actual evidence, not vibes. If you cannot source the evidence, say so.

### Step 4: Identify the Key Relationship Risk

Name the single human variable most likely to break this deal. Examples:

- The seller's internal politics could collapse the sale
- The anchor tenant's CEO is leaving and the next CEO might reverse the commitment
- The city planning director retires mid-entitlement
- The sponsor's marriage is in trouble
- The operating partner has a gambling problem

If you cannot name this risk, you have not done the human diligence.

### Step 5: Build the Relationship Plan

For the two or three most important actors, state:

- The current state of the relationship
- The desired state at closing and at key milestones
- The specific actions (calls, meetings, meals, meaningful moments) that move from current to desired
- Who on your side owns that relationship

Relationships are built, not found. If nobody on the team owns a key relationship, nobody is building it.

### Step 6: Run the Courage Check

Iger: integrity is knowing what the right call is; courage is making it when it costs you. Ask:

- What is the hard conversation nobody on the deal team wants to have yet? Who has to have it and when?
- What is the gracious-but-firm message to a party whose expectations need to be recalibrated?
- What is the deal-breaking behavior that, if observed, triggers an immediate walk — and does everyone on the team know it in advance?

If the hard conversation is unplanned, the deal will drift toward the soft one.

### Step 7: Write the Human Verdict

> "Principal actors: [list]. Trust map: [summary]. Character read on primary counterparty: [one paragraph]. Key relationship risk: [named]. Relationship plan owner: [name]. Hard conversation required: [what and when]. [Proceed / proceed after relationship step / walk]."

## When to Apply

Every deal with a material counterparty, partner, key tenant, or public-sector interaction — which is essentially every deal larger than a small stabilized asset bought from a motivated seller.

## When Not to Apply

Fully programmatic transactions where the counterparty is fungible (buying a unit in a generic condo from a developer, buying loans in a securitized pool). Even then, note the absence and flag it as a source of commoditization.

## Integration With Other Skills

This skill handles human and relationship survivability. Combine with Drucker's enterprise audit (operating team dynamics), Corcoran's narrative audit (how the story lands with buyers and the public), and Taleb's ruin mapping (human blowups are a major ruin path).
