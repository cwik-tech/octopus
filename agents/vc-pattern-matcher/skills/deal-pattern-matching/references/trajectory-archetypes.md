# Trajectory Archetypes

## Purpose

Detailed success and failure archetypes for startup trajectories. Each archetype includes the structural pattern, historical examples, key enabling conditions, and what to watch for when pattern-matching against a current deal.

---

## Success Archetypes

### 1. The Compounding Machine

**Pattern:** Steady 2-3x annual growth, improving margins, expanding customer base. Not flashy, but relentless. The company never has a breakout quarter — it just never has a bad one.

**Historical examples:** Veeva Systems, ServiceNow, Atlassian, Intuit, Autodesk (modern era)

**Structural signature:**
- Revenue growth: 40-80% YoY, remarkably consistent quarter to quarter
- NRR: >120% — existing customers expand reliably
- Gross margins: >75% and improving
- Sales efficiency: high magic number, low burn multiple
- Customer base: diversified, no concentration

**Enabling conditions:**
- Large, mission-critical problem (customers can't stop using the product)
- High switching costs once embedded
- Clear expansion path within each customer
- Product improves incrementally (not dependent on breakthroughs)

**What to watch for in current deal:** Consistent cohort behavior. If early cohorts are compounding, the pattern may hold. If growth is lumpy and driven by large contracts, it's not this archetype.

### 2. The Network Effect Flywheel

**Pattern:** Painfully slow early growth while building critical mass, then explosive acceleration as network effects compound. Winner-take-most outcome.

**Historical examples:** Uber, Airbnb, LinkedIn, Facebook, Etsy (early era)

**Structural signature:**
- Growth: sub-linear early, then super-linear (hockey stick)
- User retention: improves dramatically as network grows
- Supply-demand balance: initially unbalanced, then self-reinforcing
- Multi-homing: decreases as network matures
- Geographic/market expansion: sequential, market by market

**Enabling conditions:**
- Clear two-sided (or multi-sided) value creation
- Significant cold-start problem (the hard part is getting started)
- Defensible once critical mass is reached
- Market structure supports winner-take-most

**What to watch for:** Is the cold start being solved? Are there signs of network effects activating (retention improving with scale, organic growth accelerating)?

### 3. The Land-and-Expand

**Pattern:** Small initial contract within each customer, then rapid expansion over time. Net Revenue Retention (NRR) >130% is the defining metric.

**Historical examples:** Slack, Datadog, Snowflake, Twilio, HashiCorp

**Structural signature:**
- Initial ACV: small relative to total customer budget ($5K-$50K)
- Expansion: 3-10x revenue per customer over 3 years
- NRR: >130%, driven by usage growth and module adoption
- Logo churn: low (<5% annual)
- Revenue churn: negative (expansion exceeds churn)

**Enabling conditions:**
- Usage-based or modular pricing that grows with customer value
- Product serves multiple teams or use cases within the customer
- Low initial friction (easy to start, hard to leave)
- Technical product adopted bottom-up (developers, analysts, operators)

**What to watch for:** Cohort expansion curves. Are early cohorts still expanding? Is expansion organic or sales-driven?

### 4. The Category Creator

**Pattern:** Defines a new product category. Educates the market. Becomes the default in the category they created.

**Historical examples:** Salesforce (cloud CRM (Customer Relationship Management)), HubSpot (inbound marketing), Zuora (subscription management), Gainsight (customer success)

**Structural signature:**
- Early sales cycles: very long (educating buyers)
- Brand: becomes synonymous with the category
- Market: grows as awareness increases
- Competition: initially none, then many imitators
- Pricing power: high (category leader sets the standard)

**Enabling conditions:**
- The problem exists but has no recognized category of solution
- Founder has strong storytelling and market-shaping ability
- Enough early adopters willing to try something new
- The timing is right (market is ready for the category to exist)

**Risk:** Category creation is expensive and slow. If the market doesn't adopt the category, the company educates the market for free and a faster follower captures the value.

### 5. The Platform Shift Rider

**Pattern:** Rides a major technology transition. Old world → new world. The company is the default solution in the new paradigm.

**Historical examples:** AWS (on-premise → cloud), Shopify (retail → e-commerce), Zoom (in-person → remote), CrowdStrike (legacy → cloud security)

**Structural signature:**
- Growth: accelerates with the platform shift
- TAM: expands as the shift progresses
- Competition: legacy players struggle to adapt
- Pricing: initially lower than legacy, then increases with market power
- Customer base: starts with digital-native, then captures legacy converts

**Enabling conditions:**
- A genuine platform shift is underway (not incremental improvement)
- Legacy solutions are poorly suited to the new paradigm
- The company is purpose-built for the new paradigm
- Shift timeline is 5-10 years (long enough to build a large business)

### 6. The Vertical Dominator

**Pattern:** Owns a single vertical completely. Deep product, high switching costs, limited TAM (Total Addressable Market) but dominant market share.

**Historical examples:** Toast (restaurants), Procore (construction), Veeva (life sciences), Clio (legal)

**Structural signature:**
- Market share: dominant in a single vertical (>30%)
- Product depth: far deeper than horizontal alternatives
- Switching costs: very high (the product runs the business)
- Revenue per customer: high and growing
- TAM: limited but fully captured

**Enabling conditions:**
- The vertical has specific needs that horizontal solutions don't address
- Regulatory or workflow complexity creates barriers to entry
- Customers in the vertical value depth over breadth
- Adjacent verticals create expansion opportunity after domination

## Failure Archetypes

*(Detailed in failure-mode-catalog.md)*

### Quick Reference

| Archetype | Key Signal | Timeline to Failure |
|---|---|---|
| Premature Scaling | Burn multiple >3x, hiring ahead of revenue | 12-24 months |
| Fundraising Treadmill | Rising valuation without improving metrics | 2-4 rounds |
| Co-Founder Breakup | Team conflict, departures, equity disputes | 6-18 months |
| The Market That Wasn't | Long sales cycles, low willingness to pay | 18-36 months |
| Feature, Not a Product | Platform launches competing feature | 6-12 months |
| Channel Dependency Death | >70% from one channel, algorithm changes | 3-12 months |
