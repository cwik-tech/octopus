# Startup Failure Mode Catalog

## Purpose

Comprehensive catalog of startup failure modes with early warning signals, structural causes, and what investors should look for during diligence.

---

## Failure Mode 1 — Premature Scaling

### Pattern
The company raises a large round and immediately scales hiring, marketing, and infrastructure before achieving product-market fit. Burn rate skyrockets without corresponding revenue acceleration.

### Early Warning Signals
- Burn multiple >3x (spending $3+ for every $1 of new ARR)
- Headcount growing faster than revenue (>2x rate)
- Multiple product pivots within 12 months
- Marketing spend increasing without improving unit economics
- "We're investing in growth" as explanation for deteriorating metrics

### Structural Cause
Venture capital creates a paradox: the capital to scale is available before the knowledge of what to scale exists. Founders under pressure to deploy capital and show growth hire too fast and spend too broadly.

### Historical Examples
Fab.com (raised $336M, pivoted multiple times, sold for ~$15M), Jawbone (raised $930M, never found product-market fit)

### Red Flags in Diligence
- Team size >30 with <$500K MRR
- Hiring plan assumes 3x+ headcount growth in next 12 months
- No clear evidence of repeatability in customer acquisition
- Founder describes growth plan in terms of spending, not in terms of customer behavior

## Failure Mode 2 — The Fundraising Treadmill

### Pattern
The company raises round after round at increasing valuations without fundamentally improving its unit economics or reaching sustainability. Each round buys 18-24 months of runway, but the company is no closer to profitability. Eventually, the music stops.

### Early Warning Signals
- Valuation grows 2-3x per round but key metrics (retention, unit economics) improve <50%
- "Bridge rounds" or "extensions" between major rounds
- Capital efficiency (revenue per $ raised) declining with each round
- Founder focuses fundraising milestones over operational milestones
- Governance complexity increasing (multiple SAFE caps, preference stacks)

### Structural Cause
In bullish capital markets, fundraising ability can substitute for business fundamentals. As long as new investors are willing to invest at higher valuations, the lack of sustainable economics is hidden.

### Historical Examples
WeWork (raised $12.8B before failed IPO revealed fundamental unit economics issues), Quibi (raised $1.75B on thesis alone)

### Red Flags in Diligence
- Company has raised >3 rounds without clear path to break-even
- Revenue per $ raised is declining with each round
- The round narrative is about "next round" milestones, not operational milestones
- Existing investors declining pro-rata participation

## Failure Mode 3 — Co-Founder Breakup

### Pattern
Co-founders disagree on direction, equity, or roles. One or more founders depart, creating a leadership vacuum, equity disputes, and organizational trauma.

### Early Warning Signals
- Equity split described as "still being finalized"
- One co-founder dominates all external communication
- Divergent visions for the company (one wants lifestyle, one wants venture scale)
- Co-founders who met recently and haven't worked together under stress
- Key technical founder is disengaged or working part-time

### Structural Cause
Founding a company is the highest-stress professional relationship most people will ever have. Without strong pre-existing trust, aligned incentives, and clear role definitions, the relationship fractures under pressure.

### Historical Examples
Zipcar (co-founder conflict led to premature departure of key founder), many unreported cases (co-founder breakups are often kept private)

### Red Flags in Diligence
- When asked "what happens if you disagree," founders look uncomfortable
- Vesting schedules are non-standard or absent
- One founder has significantly more equity without clear justification
- References describe the team as "complicated" or "personality-driven"

## Failure Mode 4 — The Market That Wasn't

### Pattern
The problem is real, the product works, but the market won't pay enough (or at all) to support a venture-scale business. The startup builds a good product for a market that doesn't exist at venture scale.

### Early Warning Signals
- Sales cycles are 2-3x longer than projected
- Customer willingness to pay is significantly below pricing assumptions
- "Pilots" and "POCs" that don't convert to paid contracts
- Customers describe the product as "nice to have" rather than "must have"
- ACV is too low to support the sales motion required

### Structural Cause
Market sizing based on top-down analysis can create phantom TAMs. Just because an industry spends $50B doesn't mean $5B of it is addressable by a startup's product. Bottom-up analysis (how many customers × realistic ACV) often reveals a much smaller real market.

### Historical Examples
Segway (real technology, phantom mass market), Google Glass (first iteration — real technology, no consumer market)

### Red Flags in Diligence
- Market size is only justified top-down, not bottom-up
- No customer is paying full price (all pilots, discounts, or free)
- The "why now" is weak — the problem has existed for years without a solution, suggesting customers have adequate workarounds
- Founder hasn't talked to 50+ potential customers

## Failure Mode 5 — Feature, Not a Product

### Pattern
What appears to be a standalone product is actually a feature that a larger platform will eventually build or acquire. The startup creates value that accrues to the platform, not to the startup.

### Early Warning Signals
- Core value proposition can be described in one sentence
- Product is tightly coupled to a single platform (Salesforce, AWS, Google, Slack)
- Platform has history of shipping competing features
- Integration with the platform is the primary value (not standalone value)
- No data moat or network effects to defend against platform replication

### Structural Cause
Platforms eventually absorb high-value features. The question is timing — can the startup build enough scale and switching costs before the platform replicates the feature?

### Historical Examples
TweetDeck (acquired by Twitter for feature absorption), many Salesforce ecosystem companies that were obsoleted by native features

### Red Flags in Diligence
- Company's roadmap is essentially "add more features on top of [platform]"
- Platform has announced features that overlap with the startup's core product
- Customers describe the product as "a better version of [platform feature]"
- The company's API dependency is >80% on a single platform

## Failure Mode 6 — Channel Dependency Death

### Pattern
Growth depends on a single acquisition channel. When that channel changes (algorithm update, cost increase, policy change), growth collapses.

### Early Warning Signals
- >70% of new customers from a single channel
- Rapid growth that coincides with an algorithm or platform change
- CAC increasing consistently (channel is becoming more competitive)
- No organic or word-of-mouth growth component
- Company can't describe what happens if their primary channel disappears

### Structural Cause
Channels are rented, not owned. Facebook, Google, Apple, and other platforms change their rules regularly. A business built on someone else's platform is dependent on that platform's priorities.

### Historical Examples
Zynga (Facebook algorithm changes), many D2C (Direct-to-Consumer) brands (Facebook ad cost increases), HouseParty (App Store algorithm changes)

### Red Flags in Diligence
- Customer acquisition breakdown shows >70% single-channel
- Company has no content marketing, community, or organic acquisition strategy
- CAC has increased >50% in the last 12 months
- Company's competitive advantage is "we're better at [channel]" rather than "we have a better product"

## Failure Mode 7 — Regulatory Ambush

### Pattern
The company operates in a regulatory gray zone. When regulation arrives, it either outlaws the business model, imposes costs that destroy unit economics, or creates compliance requirements that slow growth.

### Early Warning Signals
- Company describes regulation as "unclear" or "evolving"
- No legal counsel with regulatory expertise on team or advisory board
- Competitors are being investigated or fined
- Regulatory bodies are publicly discussing the space
- Company's legal budget is minimal relative to regulatory exposure

### Structural Cause
In emerging sectors, regulation often lags innovation. Companies that move fast in regulatory gray zones can be caught when regulation catches up.

### Historical Examples
Many crypto/DeFi companies (SEC enforcement), some fintech companies (state money transmitter requirements), some healthtech companies (FDA classification disputes)

## Failure Mode 8 — Talent Exodus

### Pattern
Key employees (especially engineering talent) leave, creating a capability vacuum. Recruiting replacements is difficult because the company's reputation has degraded or the opportunity is no longer attractive.

### Early Warning Signals
- Key technical hires from the last 18 months have left
- Glassdoor reviews mention culture, management, or direction concerns
- Recruiting velocity has slowed despite increasing headcount targets
- Founder dismisses departures as "not a good fit" without reflection
- No VP-level technical leadership (only the founder)

### Structural Cause
Talent follows opportunity, culture, and leadership quality. When any of these deteriorate, the best people leave first (because they have the most options). The remaining team faces increasing workload and declining morale, creating a negative spiral.
