# Six Thinking Hats: Post-Decomposition Evaluation Technique

**When to use:** After completing a problem decomposition and forming an initial plan, cycle through the Six Thinking Hats to stress-test your solution from multiple perspectives. Use this as a supplementary evaluation phase when high stakes or irreversible decisions are involved.

**Why this works with decomposition:** Decomposition breaks a problem into logical pieces, but can miss emotional, creative, or critical dimensions. The hats force systematic perspective-shifting to expose blind spots.

## The Six Hats Framework

### White Hat: Facts & Information
**Focus:** Objective data only. No opinions or speculation.

**Apply to your plan:**
- Verify each component has sufficient data support
- Identify missing information: "What facts do we lack?"
- Check assumptions against evidence: "Which decomposed steps rest on untested assumptions?"
- Flag gaps: "If we're missing X data, can we proceed safely?"

### Red Hat: Emotion & Intuition
**Focus:** Gut feeling, hunches, emotional reactions—without justification needed.

**Apply to your plan:**
- Ask: "Does this feel right intuitively, even if logic says yes?"
- Capture stakeholder emotional concerns: "Will people trust this?"
- Surface doubt: "Where do I feel uneasy about this decomposition?"
- Note: Red Hat insight doesn't override white facts, but reveals what might derail implementation.

### Black Hat: Critical Judgment
**Focus:** Identify flaws, risks, and worst-case scenarios.

**Apply to your plan:**
- Stress-test each decomposed component: "What can go wrong here?"
- Find logical contradictions: "Does step B really follow from step A?"
- Ask the hard question: "Why might this decomposition be wrong?"
- Challenge assumptions: "Is this component actually independent of that one?"

### Yellow Hat: Optimism & Benefits
**Focus:** Best-case thinking. What's the payoff?

**Apply to your plan:**
- Highlight upsides of each component: "If this works, what's the benefit?"
- Identify synergies: "Do these decomposed pieces amplify each other?"
- Challenge black-hat pessimism: "What if this actually works better than expected?"
- Uncover hidden opportunities: "Does solving this unlock other problems?"

### Green Hat: Creativity & Alternatives
**Focus:** Generate new ideas, variations, provocations.

**Apply to your plan:**
- Ask: "Is there a different way to decompose this problem?"
- Suggest alternatives to stuck components: "What if we inverted this step?"
- Combine solutions in novel ways: "Could we parallelize these tasks?"
- Lateral leap: "What would a completely different domain solve this way?"

### Blue Hat: Process Control & Meta
**Focus:** Manage the thinking itself. When to switch hats, what we've learned, next steps.

**Apply to your plan:**
- Summarize findings from each hat: "What did we learn from red that changes white?"
- Identify the dominant issue: "What's the single biggest risk the hats revealed?"
- Decide: "Does this decomposition proceed as-is, or do we revise?"
- Define accountability: "Who owns each hat's concern going forward?"

## Implementation Flow

1. **Decompose first.** Settle on your primary decomposition before putting on hats.
2. **White Hat.** Verify data foundations of each component.
3. **Red Hat.** Capture emotional/trust concerns.
4. **Black Hat.** Attack the plan ruthlessly.
5. **Yellow Hat.** Counter-argue black's pessimism.
6. **Green Hat.** Sketch alternatives or improvements.
7. **Blue Hat.** Synthesize and decide on next action (proceed, revise, abandon).

## For AI Agents

When reasoning through a problem:
- **Cycle deliberately.** Don't wear all hats at once—sequential perspective-taking prevents emotional reasoning from dominating.
- **Document each hat's output.** This creates an audit trail: "We considered X, found risk Y, yellow reframed it as Z."
- **Black-Yellow pairs.** Let black expose risks, then yellow propose mitigations. This prevents both nihilism and blind optimism.
- **Blue Hat decides.** After cycling through all hats, use blue to synthesize: "Given all perspectives, here's our decision and why."

**When NOT to use:** For low-stakes, time-sensitive decisions, six hats adds overhead. Use for complex, reversible problems where stakeholder buy-in matters.
