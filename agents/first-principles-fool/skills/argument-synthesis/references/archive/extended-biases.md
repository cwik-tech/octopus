# Extended Cognitive Biases Catalogue

Supplementary reference. The primary file (`references/cognitive-biases.md`) covers the 10 highest-frequency biases for consulting deliberations. This file catalogues additional biases that surface in specific contexts.

---

## Decision and Planning Biases

### Planning Fallacy

Underestimating the time, cost, and risk of future actions while overestimating their benefits. Nearly universal in project scoping.

**Detect:** Timelines based on best-case scenarios. No buffer for unknowns. "We'll have this done in six weeks" with no historical reference.

**Counter:** "What happened last time we estimated a project like this? What's the base rate for projects of this scope?"

### Optimism Bias

Overweighting the probability of positive outcomes and underweighting negative ones. Different from planning fallacy — this is about *outcomes*, not just *timelines*.

**Detect:** Revenue projections that assume everything goes right. Risk sections that list risks but assign low probability to all of them.

**Counter:** "Run the pessimistic scenario. If the two biggest risks both materialize, what happens?"

### Commitment Escalation (Escalation of Commitment)

Doubling down on a position after publicly stating it, even when new evidence suggests reversing. Common in multi-round deliberations where agents have taken stances.

**Detect:** An agent defends a position with increasing intensity despite mounting counter-evidence. They reframe challenges rather than updating.

**Counter:** "You've been consistent on this. If you were arguing the opposite side, what would your strongest point be?"

### Zero-Risk Bias

Preferring to eliminate a small risk entirely rather than making a larger reduction in a bigger risk. Leads to misallocation of effort.

**Detect:** "We need to make sure this edge case never happens" when a different, larger risk gets less attention.

**Counter:** "Which risk has the higher expected cost — the one we're eliminating or the one we're tolerating?"

### Neglect of Probability

Ignoring the actual likelihood of events and responding only to their magnitude. A catastrophic but near-impossible event gets the same weight as a moderate but likely one.

**Detect:** Arguments driven entirely by "what if" worst cases without probability attached.

**Counter:** "How likely is this? One in ten? One in a thousand? The response should be proportional."

## Communication and Knowledge Biases

### Curse of Knowledge

Assuming others know what you know. Once you understand something, it's hard to imagine not understanding it. Particularly relevant when communicating with clients.

**Detect:** Jargon-heavy recommendations. Skipping foundational context. "Obviously we need to migrate to microservices" — obvious to whom?

**Counter:** "Would the client's CFO understand this recommendation without our context? What would we need to explain first?"

### Narrative Fallacy

Fitting messy, complex data into a clean, compelling story. Consultants are especially prone — storytelling is a core skill, which makes this bias dangerous.

**Detect:** The argument is suspiciously neat. Every data point supports the thesis. No loose ends, no contradictions.

**Counter:** "This is a clean narrative. What data did we leave out because it didn't fit? What's the messier but more accurate version?"

### Illusion of Explanatory Depth

Believing you understand something deeply when you actually understand it superficially. Exposed when asked to explain the mechanism step by step.

**Detect:** Confident assertions about how something works, followed by vagueness when pressed on specifics.

**Counter:** "Walk me through the exact mechanism. Step by step — how does X cause Y?"

### Information Bias

Seeking additional information even when it cannot affect the decision. Leads to analysis paralysis.

**Detect:** "We need more data before we can decide" when the decision would be the same regardless of the data's outcome.

**Counter:** "If the data came back showing X, would you change your recommendation? If not, we don't need it."

## Social and Group Biases

### Bandwagon Effect

Adopting a position because others have, not because of its merits. Different from groupthink — this is about external trends and market consensus, not internal team dynamics.

**Detect:** "Everyone in the industry is doing this" as the primary justification. No independent analysis of whether it's right for this situation.

**Counter:** "Is the industry right, or is this a herd? What percentage of companies doing this are actually seeing results?"

### In-Group Bias

Favoring ideas from people perceived as part of "our group" (same team, same background, same company) over outsiders.

**Detect:** Dismissing external research or competitor approaches while accepting internal opinions without the same scrutiny.

**Counter:** "Apply the same evidence standard to this internal recommendation as you would to a competitor's strategy."

### Halo Effect

Letting one positive trait of a person, company, or product color the overall assessment. A company that succeeded in one domain is assumed competent in all domains.

**Detect:** "Company X is great at marketing, so their product strategy must be sound too." One strength illuminating unrelated areas.

**Counter:** "That's their marketing strength. What's the evidence for their product strategy specifically?"

## Estimation and Prediction Biases

### Base Rate Neglect

Ignoring the general probability of an event (the base rate) in favor of specific but less reliable information.

**Detect:** "Our startup will succeed because we have a great team" — ignoring that ~90% of startups fail regardless of team quality.

**Counter:** "What's the base rate for this kind of initiative? Of 100 companies that tried this, how many succeeded?"

### Hindsight Bias

After an event has occurred, believing you "knew it all along." Distorts post-mortems and lessons-learned sessions.

**Detect:** "I always thought that acquisition was a mistake" — said only after the acquisition failed.

**Counter:** "What did we actually document at the time of the decision? What was our confidence level then?"

### Overconfidence Effect

Overestimating the accuracy of your own judgments. Particularly dangerous in experts, who are often more overconfident than novices.

**Detect:** Predictions stated without uncertainty ranges. "Revenue will be $5M" instead of "$3M-7M."

**Counter:** "What's the confidence interval? Give me the range where you're 80% sure the answer falls."

### Recency Bias

Overweighting recent events relative to older ones. Last quarter's performance dominates the analysis even when longer trends tell a different story.

**Detect:** Arguments built primarily on the last 3 months of data when 3 years are available.

**Counter:** "What does the 3-year trend show? Is last quarter representative or an anomaly?"

## Consulting-Specific Biases

### Hammer-Nail Bias (Law of the Instrument)

When your primary tool is a hammer, everything looks like a nail. Consultants with a specialty tend to diagnose every problem as needing that specialty.

**Detect:** A technology consultant recommending technology solutions to what might be a people or process problem.

**Counter:** "If we couldn't use [your specialty] here, what would the solution look like?"

### Complexity Bias

Preferring complex solutions over simple ones. Complex solutions feel more sophisticated and justify higher fees, but simple solutions are often more effective.

**Detect:** A multi-phase transformation program proposed for a problem that might be solved by changing one process.

**Counter:** "What's the simplest version of this that could work? What's the minimum viable intervention?"

### Client Pleasing Bias

Telling the client what they want to hear rather than what they need to hear. Particularly acute when the engagement is at risk or the relationship is new.

**Detect:** Recommendations that align suspiciously well with what the client already believes or has publicly committed to.

**Counter:** "Is this what the data says, or what the client wants to hear? Would we make the same recommendation if the client wanted the opposite?"
