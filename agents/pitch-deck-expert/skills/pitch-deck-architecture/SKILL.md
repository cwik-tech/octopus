# Pitch Deck Architecture

## Purpose

Create the strategic structure of a pitch deck before slide polish begins. This skill turns raw business information into a clear presentation argument with a defined audience, narrative arc, slide sequence, and evidence plan.

## When To Use

Use this skill when:

- Creating a new pitch deck from scratch.
- Rebuilding an existing deck that feels unclear, bloated, or unfocused.
- Choosing slide order and narrative flow.
- Translating a business idea into a persuasive presentation.
- Reviewing whether a deck actually supports the intended decision.

## Core Principle

A pitch deck is a sequenced argument. It should move the audience from their current belief state to the belief state required for action.

Do not begin with slide design. Begin with audience movement.

## Step 1 — Diagnose the Deck Situation

Answer these questions before outlining slides:

1. **Audience:** Who will read or hear this deck?
2. **Decision:** What decision should they make afterward?
3. **Context:** Is this a cold send, live meeting, follow-up, board discussion, investor meeting, client proposal, or partnership discussion?
4. **Stage:** Is the company or offer idea-stage, early traction, growth-stage, mature, or turnaround?
5. **Primary doubt:** What will the audience doubt most?
6. **Primary proof:** What evidence best reduces that doubt?
7. **Ask:** What is the concrete next step?

## Step 2 — Choose the Narrative Spine

Select the best narrative spine for the deck.

### Problem-Solution-Proof-Ask

Best for fundraising, new products, and sales decks.

Flow:

1. The audience recognizes the problem.
2. The solution feels specific and differentiated.
3. Proof makes the claim credible.
4. The ask feels like the obvious next step.

### Change-Insight-Response

Best for strategy, transformation, and executive decks.

Flow:

1. The world has changed.
2. This creates a specific implication.
3. The proposed response is necessary and timely.

### Opportunity-Capability-Return

Best for investment, partnership, and client proposal decks.

Flow:

1. There is a valuable opportunity.
2. The team or company is unusually positioned to capture it.
3. The expected return justifies action.

### Pain-Cost-Relief

Best for B2B sales decks.

Flow:

1. The buyer has a costly problem.
2. The current workaround is inadequate.
3. The offer provides a credible, lower-friction path to relief.

## Step 3 — Define Slide Jobs

Each slide must have one job. Common slide jobs include:

- Establish context.
- Name the problem.
- Quantify pain or opportunity.
- Introduce the solution.
- Explain how it works.
- Prove demand.
- Show traction.
- Demonstrate differentiation.
- Explain business model.
- Establish credibility.
- Clarify implementation.
- Present economics.
- Make the ask.

If a slide has more than one primary job, split it or simplify it.

## Step 4 — Build the Deck Arc

A strong deck usually follows this rhythm:

1. **Hook:** Why this matters now.
2. **Problem:** What is broken, painful, expensive, slow, risky, or newly possible.
3. **Audience relevance:** Why this specific audience should care.
4. **Solution:** What is being proposed.
5. **Mechanism:** How it works.
6. **Proof:** Why the audience should believe it.
7. **Differentiation:** Why this option over alternatives.
8. **Economics or impact:** Why action is worth it.
9. **Plan:** What happens next.
10. **Ask:** What decision or commitment is requested.

## Step 5 — Write Action Titles

Prefer slide titles that make claims, not labels.

Weak label titles:

- Market
- Product
- Competition
- Team
- Financials

Stronger action titles:

- Independent clinics lose revenue because intake is still manual
- Our workflow cuts intake time by 42% without replacing the EHR
- The wedge is urgent care, but the platform expands across outpatient care
- Three pilots show buyers will pay for speed, not just automation

A reader should understand the story by reading only the slide titles.

## Step 6 — Match Evidence to Claims

For every major claim, identify the needed proof.

| Claim Type | Strong Evidence | Weak Evidence |
|-----------|-----------------|---------------|
| Customer pain | Interviews, usage data, churn, cost, time lost | Anecdotes with no pattern |
| Market opportunity | Bottom-up market sizing, spend data, buyer count | Generic TAM screenshots |
| Traction | Revenue, pilots, retention, pipeline, usage growth | Vanity signups |
| Differentiation | Feature comparison, workflow advantage, proprietary data, switching cost | “Better UX” without proof |
| Economics | Pricing, margins, CAC/LTV logic, payback | Unsupported projections |
| Team credibility | Relevant wins, domain access, execution track record | Generic bios |

## Step 7 — Cut Redundancy

Remove or merge slides that repeat the same message. Common redundancies:

- Problem and market slides that say the same thing.
- Product and how-it-works slides that both describe features.
- Traction and validation slides that repeat the same proof.
- Team and credibility slides that duplicate biography.

```markdown
|---|-------|-----|-------------|-----------------|

### Storyline Summary
[3-5 sentences]

- [Evidence gaps]
```

## Quality Bar

The deck architecture is strong when:

- The audience and decision are explicit.
- The slide sequence has momentum.
- Every slide has one job.
- The strongest proof appears before skepticism hardens.
- The ask is clear.
- The deck can be understood from slide titles alone.
