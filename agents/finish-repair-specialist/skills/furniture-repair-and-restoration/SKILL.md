# Furniture Repair and Restoration

## Assess Before Intervening

Document the piece's construction, materials, condition, finish history, provenance or sentimental value, previous repairs, hardware, and signs of active damage. Photograph and label parts before disassembly.

Classify each issue as:

- **Structural:** loose joints, broken members, failed glue blocks, split components, compromised load paths.
- **Cosmetic:** scratches, dents, water rings, color loss, finish wear, minor chips.
- **Material stability:** veneer lifting, checking, insect damage, corrosion, moisture movement, ongoing cracking.
- **Preservation-sensitive:** original finishes, labels, patina, historic hardware, maker marks, or evidence that contributes to value.

## Restoration Principles

1. Preserve sound original material, finish, hardware, labels, and patina where practical.
2. Use minimum effective intervention. Clean, consolidate, touch up, and locally repair before full stripping or replacement.
3. Correct causes, not only visible symptoms. Moisture, failed joinery, unstable veneer, or movement must be resolved before cosmetic work.
4. Match wood species, grain direction, veneer thickness, color, sheen, and hardware style as closely as feasible.
5. Prefer repair approaches that are serviceable and reversible where the piece's value warrants it.
6. Do not disassemble joints that can be stabilized safely without destructive force.

## Common Repair Approaches

- **Loose chairs and joints:** Determine whether old adhesive must be removed and whether parts can be separated safely; repair fit and load path rather than injecting glue blindly.
- **Dents:** Consider controlled moisture and heat only for compressed fibers, not torn fibers, veneer, or heat-sensitive finishes.
- **Scratches and finish loss:** Clean first; determine whether the damage is in wax, finish, color, or wood before touch-up.
- **Veneer:** Stabilize loose veneer, retain fragments, repair substrate defects, and patch only with grain and figure matched material.
- **Water or heat marks:** Identify whether the mark is in surface contamination, finish film, color layer, or wood before attempting abrasion or refinishing.

## Stop Conditions

Recommend a conservator, qualified restorer, or specialist when the piece may have substantial historical or monetary value, has hazardous finishes or coatings, shows active insect or mold damage, or requires irreversible intervention beyond the hobbyist's capability.

## Output

Provide a condition assessment, repair priority order, preservation recommendation, materials and method, finish-integration plan, and explicit risks or referral triggers.
