# Divergent Thinking Techniques: Full Protocols

## SCAMPER

SCAMPER is a structured creativity checklist. Apply each prompt to the existing product, process, or approach:

### The Seven Prompts

**S — Substitute:** What component, material, person, or process could be replaced?
- "What if we replaced manual review with automated scoring?"
- "What if customers served themselves instead of going through sales?"
- "What if we used open-source tools instead of licensed software?"

**C — Combine:** What elements could be merged, bundled, or integrated?
- "What if onboarding and first value delivery were the same step?"
- "What if billing and usage reporting were combined into one dashboard?"
- "What if two departments shared resources instead of each maintaining their own?"

**A — Adapt:** What existing solution from another domain could be adapted here?
- "How do streaming services handle content recommendation? Could we adapt that for product discovery?"
- "How does Uber match supply and demand in real time? Could we apply that to resource allocation?"

**M — Modify / Magnify / Minimize:** What could be made bigger, smaller, faster, slower, more frequent, less frequent?
- "What if we released daily instead of quarterly?"
- "What if the product had 3 features instead of 30?"
- "What if meetings were 10 minutes instead of 60?"

**P — Put to Other Uses:** Could this product, skill, or asset serve a different purpose?
- "Our customer data is used for personalization — could it also power a benchmarking service?"
- "Our support knowledge base serves customers — could it train new employees?"

**E — Eliminate:** What could be removed entirely without losing core value?
- "What if there were no signup process?"
- "What if we eliminated the approval step?"
- "What features do fewer than 5% of users touch? What if they didn't exist?"

**R — Reverse / Rearrange:** What if the order, direction, or structure were inverted?
- "What if customers paid us before we built anything?" (Pre-sales, crowdfunding.)
- "What if support contacted customers before problems occurred?" (Proactive support.)
- "What if junior team members reviewed senior work?" (Reverse mentoring.)

### SCAMPER Tips
- Apply ALL seven prompts, not just the ones that feel natural. The uncomfortable prompts often yield the best ideas.
- Spend maximum 3 minutes per prompt. Speed prevents over-filtering.
- Generate at least 2 ideas per prompt (14+ total).

## Constraint Removal

### Protocol

1. **List all constraints** affecting the problem. Include:
   - Budget limits
   - Timeline deadlines
   - Technology limitations
   - Regulatory requirements
   - Team size or skill gaps
   - Organizational politics
   - Physical/geographic constraints
   - Customer expectations

2. **Remove one constraint at a time.** For each removed constraint, ask:
   - "If [constraint] didn't exist, what would we do?"
   - "What becomes possible when [constraint] is removed?"
   - Generate 2-3 ideas per removed constraint.

3. **Combine constraint removals.** Remove 2-3 constraints simultaneously:
   - "If we had unlimited budget AND no timeline, what would we build?"
   - "If regulatory requirements didn't apply AND we could hire anyone, what approach would we take?"

4. **Work backward to feasibility.** For each "impossible" idea:
   - What's the kernel of value in this idea?
   - Can a constrained version of this idea still capture most of the value?
   - Which constraints are actually softer than assumed? (Budget is often more flexible than believed. Timelines are often arbitrary.)

### Why It Works
Constraints are often internalized so deeply that they become invisible. Explicitly removing them surfaces assumptions the team didn't know they were making.

## Analogical Transfer

### Protocol

1. **Abstract the problem.** Strip domain-specific details to reveal the underlying structure.
   - "We need to match supply and demand in real time with variable pricing" (not "we need to price our SaaS (Software as a Service) product").
   - "We need to coordinate thousands of independent agents toward a shared goal without central control" (not "we need to manage our remote team").

2. **Find source domains.** Identify 2-3 domains that solve the abstracted problem:
   - Nature: How do ant colonies, immune systems, or ecosystems solve coordination, adaptation, or resource allocation?
   - Other industries: How do airlines, hospitals, financial markets, or logistics companies handle similar challenges?
   - Historical: How was this type of problem solved before modern technology?

3. **Extract the mechanism.** What's the core principle that makes the source domain's solution work?
   - Ant colonies use pheromone trails (local information, decentralized coordination).
   - Airlines use dynamic pricing (perishable inventory, demand-responsive pricing).
   - Open-source projects use modular architecture (independent contribution, shared standard).

4. **Transfer and adapt.** Apply the mechanism to your problem:
   - "What if our platform used a 'pheromone trail' approach — each user action leaves signals that guide other users?"
   - "What if we priced our service like airline seats — cheaper when booked early, more expensive as capacity fills?"

### Source Domain Library

| Abstracted Problem | Source Domains to Explore |
|---|---|
| Matching supply and demand | Airlines, ride-sharing, electricity markets, auction houses |
| Scaling quality without scaling people | Fast food chains, manufacturing, templating, automation |
| Coordinating without hierarchy | Open source, Wikipedia, ant colonies, market economies |
| Building trust with strangers | eBay ratings, Airbnb reviews, credit scores, accreditation |
| Handling surge/peak demand | Hospitals, cloud computing, disaster response, traffic management |
| Reducing waste in a process | Toyota Production System, lean manufacturing, nature's nutrient cycles |
| Predicting rare events | Insurance, weather forecasting, earthquake detection, epidemiology |

## Brainwriting (6-3-5 Method)

### Protocol

Best with 3-6 participants (or reasoning threads for AI agents):

1. **Setup:** Each participant gets a sheet with the problem statement and space for ideas.
2. **Round 1 (5 minutes):** Each person writes 3 ideas. No discussion.
3. **Pass sheets.** Each person receives someone else's sheet.
4. **Round 2 (5 minutes):** Read the previous ideas. Write 3 new ideas — inspired by, building on, or reacting against the previous ones.
5. **Repeat for 3-5 rounds.** Each round builds on the cumulative ideas.
6. **Collect and cluster.** Pool all ideas and move to the Clustering phase.

### Why Brainwriting Over Brainstorming

- **No anchoring:** Ideas are generated independently before being shared.
- **No dominance:** Loud voices don't suppress quiet insights.
- **Cross-pollination:** Each round exposes participants to different thinking.
- **Productivity:** 6 people × 5 rounds × 3 ideas = 90 ideas in 25 minutes.

### For AI Agents

Simulate brainwriting by generating ideas from 3 different "perspectives":
- Perspective 1: The pragmatist (what's most feasible?)
- Perspective 2: The innovator (what's most novel?)
- Perspective 3: The customer advocate (what solves the user's problem most directly?)

Then cross-pollinate: use ideas from one perspective to inspire ideas in another.

## Random Entry / Forced Association

### Protocol

1. **Select a random stimulus.** Options:
   - Random word from a dictionary
   - Random image
   - Random object in the room
   - Random Wikipedia article
   - Random industry (e.g., "How would a theme park solve this?")

2. **List attributes of the stimulus.** What are its properties, functions, characteristics?
   - "Library: organized by category, free access, quiet spaces, borrowing system, diverse collection, community hub."

3. **Force connections.** For each attribute, ask: "How could this apply to our problem?"
   - "Organized by category → What if we categorized our features by user maturity level?"
   - "Borrowing system → What if customers could 'borrow' premium features temporarily?"
   - "Community hub → What if our product had a community space where users solved each other's problems?"

4. **Develop promising connections.** Most forced connections will be absurd. That's fine. 1 in 5 will spark something genuinely useful.

### Why It Works

Random stimuli force the brain off its default path. When you're stuck in a rut, random entry provides an external kick that breaks pattern-matching and enables lateral thinking.
