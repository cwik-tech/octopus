# Common Issues — Reference

The 30 most common problems found in business consulting documents, with examples and fixes. Organised by review layer.

---

## Layer 1 — Brief Compliance

**Issue 1 — Missing section**
Example: Brief specified a "Risk Assessment" section. Draft has no such section.
Fix: Writer must draft the section, using any risk-related material in the inputs, or flag as a placeholder if inputs are insufficient.

**Issue 2 — Wrong depth**
Example: Brief specified "Long" for the Market Analysis section. Draft has two paragraphs.
Fix: Return with instruction to expand — specify what's missing (more data, more analysis, more context).

**Issue 3 — Brief ignored**
Example: Brief said "lead with the recommendation." Draft builds up to it on page 4.
Fix: Restructure — move recommendation to the opening. This is a BLOCKING issue.

**Issue 4 — Unresolved placeholder**
Example: Draft contains `[TODO: Insert Q3 churn rate]` in the Financial section.
Fix: BLOCKING until resolved. Either source the data or remove the claim that depends on it.

**Issue 5 — Purpose not delivered**
Example: Brief said the document should enable the client to choose between three vendors. Draft presents the analysis but doesn't reach a recommendation.
Fix: BLOCKING. Add a clear recommendation section with a stated preferred option and rationale.

---

## Layer 2 — Structural Logic

**Issue 6 — Unsupported major claim**
Example: "The market is growing rapidly and presents a significant opportunity." No market data provided anywhere.
Fix: Either provide the data or remove the claim. Do not let "significant opportunity" stand without evidence.

**Issue 7 — Executive summary contradicts body**
Example: Summary says "we recommend Option A." Body concludes "Option B offers the strongest risk-adjusted return."
Fix: BLOCKING. Align. The body is the source of truth — the summary must reflect it.

**Issue 8 — Circular reasoning**
Example: "Option A is the recommended path because it is the most strategically sound choice." (Why is it the most strategically sound? It's not explained.)
Fix: State the actual reasons Option A is preferred over the alternatives.

**Issue 9 — Logical leap**
Example: Evidence shows customer churn is increasing. Next sentence: "The company should therefore pivot to enterprise." The connection between these two statements is never made.
Fix: Add the intermediate reasoning — why does increasing churn in the current segment make enterprise the right response?

**Issue 10 — Sections that could be in any order**
Example: The document has five sections that feel disconnected — each could be read in isolation without any logical dependency on the others.
Fix: Add transitions. Each section should explicitly build on or refer to the previous one.

**Issue 11 — Evidence presented without implication**
Example: "Net Promoter Score (NPS) is 42." Full stop. What does this mean? Is 42 good or bad? What does it mean for the recommendation?
Fix: Add context (benchmark, prior score) and state the "so what."

**Issue 12 — Recommendation doesn't address the stated problem**
Example: Problem section identifies "high customer acquisition cost as the primary challenge." Recommendation is about "improving the product roadmap."
Fix: The recommendation must directly address the problem. If it doesn't, either the problem is wrong or the recommendation is wrong.

## Layer 3 — Clarity and Concision

**Issue 13 — Opening sentence announces instead of stating**
Example: "In this section, we will examine the key factors driving market growth."
Fix: "Three factors are driving market growth: X, Y, and Z."

**Issue 14 — The impressive nothing**
Example: "We take a comprehensive, integrated approach to driving sustainable transformation across the organisation's strategic and operational dimensions."
Fix: State what the approach actually involves. Name the components.

**Issue 15 — Double-say**
Example: Two paragraphs in the same section making the same point with different words. Often found when a writer re-explains the executive summary in the body.
Fix: Delete the weaker of the two occurrences.

**Issue 16 — Paragraph too long**
Example: A 9-sentence paragraph covering three distinct ideas.
Fix: Split into two or three focused paragraphs, each with one clear point.

**Issue 17 — Vague quantifier**
Example: "The company has seen significant growth" appears in the executive summary but the growth figure is in the body.
Fix: State the number in the executive summary. "Revenue grew 31% year-over-year."

**Issue 18 — Passive hiding the actor**
Example: "The decision was made to restructure the sales team." Who made the decision? It matters.
Fix: "The CEO approved a restructure of the sales team in Q2."

**Issue 19 — Buried conclusion**
Example: Four paragraphs of analysis followed by "therefore, we recommend Option A" as the last sentence.
Fix: Lead with the conclusion. The analysis supports it; the conclusion doesn't emerge from it.

**Issue 20 — Unnecessary qualifiers throughout**
Example: "This is quite a significant and rather substantial improvement over the previous period."
Fix: "This is a substantial improvement over the previous period." Or better: "Revenue grew 31%, up from 18% the prior year."

## Layer 4 — Style Compliance

**Issue 21 — Tone mismatch**
Example: Profile is "executive-concise" (authoritative, direct). Document reads as tentative throughout: "it might be worth considering whether perhaps the company should explore..."
Fix: Remove hedges. State conclusions directly.

**Issue 22 — Formality collapse**
Example: Document starts formally, then by page 5 includes contractions and casual constructions.
Fix: Review the second half of the document for formality drift and standardise.

**Issue 23 — Wrong person**
Example: Profile specifies third person. Document uses "we" throughout.
Fix: Systematic pass to convert first-person plural to third person.

**Issue 24 — Hedging above profile**
Example: Profile is "minimal hedging." Document contains 14 instances of "might," "could," "perhaps," or "appears to."
Fix: Convert each hedge to a direct statement unless genuine uncertainty exists.

---

## Layer 5 — Consistency

**Issue 25 — Terminology drift**
Example: "The platform" in sections 1-3, "the system" in sections 4-5, "the tool" in section 6.
Fix: Choose one term and apply it consistently throughout.

**Issue 26 — Abbreviation not defined**
Example: "CAC payback period" used in section 3 without prior definition.
Fix: First use in the document: "Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC) payback period." Subsequent uses: "CAC payback period."

**Issue 27 — Tense inconsistency**
Example: Present tense for findings in section 2, past tense for the same type of finding in section 4.
Fix: Standardise — past tense for historical data, present tense for analysis and recommendations.

**Issue 28 — Heading level inconsistency**
Example: Some subsections use H2, others use H3 for equivalent levels of hierarchy.
Fix: Standardise heading hierarchy throughout.

**Issue 29 — Number formatting inconsistency**
Example: "3 months" in one section, "three months" in another.
Fix: Apply a consistent rule (numerals for quantities with units, words for counts under ten — or whatever the style guide specifies).

**Issue 30 — Named entity inconsistency**
Example: "Acme Corporation" in the introduction, "Acme Corp" in section 3, "Acme" thereafter — with no established convention.
Fix: Define the preferred short form on first use: "Acme Corporation (Acme)" and use "Acme" consistently thereafter.
