# Argument Construction Skill

## When to Use

On every document draft. The Writer uses this skill to build logical, evidence-backed arguments that follow the framework specified in the brief or template.

---

## Argumentation Frameworks

### Framework 1 — SCR (Situation, Complication, Resolution)

**Best for:** Recommendations, proposals, strategy documents. Any document that needs the reader to understand a problem before accepting a solution.

**Structure:**

1. **Situation:** State the current reality the reader already knows or will accept without argument. Ground the reader. "The company has grown 40% year-over-year for three consecutive years, primarily through organic acquisition in the mid-market segment."
2. **Complication:** Introduce the tension — what has changed, what's at risk, what's broken, what's emerging. This is the "but" that creates urgency. "However, customer acquisition costs have doubled in the past 18 months, and the mid-market segment is showing signs of saturation."
3. **Resolution:** Present the recommendation or path forward. This must directly address the complication. "To sustain growth, the company should expand into the enterprise segment, leveraging its existing product capabilities while building a dedicated enterprise sales team."

**Common mistakes:**
- Situation too long — the reader already knows the context. Keep it to 1-2 paragraphs max.
- Complication is vague — "the market is changing" says nothing. Be specific about WHAT changed and WHY it matters.
- Resolution doesn't address the complication — the reader expects the resolution to directly solve the problem you just described. If it doesn't, your argument breaks.

**Nesting:** SCR can nest within sections. Each section of a document can follow its own mini-SCR arc while the document as a whole follows a macro-SCR structure.

### Framework 2 — Pyramid Principle (Minto)

**Best for:** Executive documents, board memos, any document where the reader wants the answer first.

**Structure:**

1. **Lead with the answer:** State your main conclusion or recommendation in the first paragraph. Do not build up to it.
2. **Group supporting arguments:** Organize your supporting points into 2-4 groups. Each group should be a distinct line of reasoning.
3. **Support each group with evidence:** Under each argument group, provide the specific data, examples, or analysis that proves the point.
4. **So what:** For each evidence point, state its implication — why this data matters for the conclusion.

**Visualization:**

```
Main conclusion
├── Argument Group A
│   ├── Evidence 1 → So what
│   ├── Evidence 2 → So what
│   └── Evidence 3 → So what
├── Argument Group B
│   ├── Evidence 1 → So what
│   └── Evidence 2 → So what
└── Argument Group C
    ├── Evidence 1 → So what
    └── Evidence 2 → So what
```

**Common mistakes:**
- Starting with background instead of the answer — executive readers will skip to the recommendation anyway. Put it first.
- Too many argument groups — 5+ groups means your thinking isn't organized. Consolidate.
- Evidence without "so what" — data doesn't speak for itself. You must state the implication.

### Framework 3 — MECE (Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive)

**Best for:** Options analysis, market segmentation, assessment categories, any document that presents a set of alternatives or categories.

**Rules:**

1. **Mutually Exclusive:** No overlap between categories. If an item could fit in two categories, your categories are wrong.
2. **Collectively Exhaustive:** All possibilities are covered. If someone can point to a case that fits no category, your framework is incomplete.

**Application in documents:**

- **Options analysis:** When presenting 3 options, ensure they represent genuinely different approaches, not variations of the same idea. Each option should have a distinct risk profile, timeline, and resource requirement.
- **Market segmentation:** When breaking down a market, ensure every potential customer fits exactly one segment.
- **Problem decomposition:** When analyzing causes of a problem, ensure your categories cover all possible causes without duplication.

**Testing MECE:**
- Pick any specific example. Does it fit in exactly one category? If it fits two, you have overlap. If it fits none, you have a gap.
- Ask: "Is there anything else?" for exhaustiveness. "Does this overlap with that?" for exclusivity.

**Common mistakes:**
- Presenting MECE categories that are technically exclusive but not meaningfully different to the reader
- Being exhaustive to the point of including irrelevant categories — MECE doesn't mean "list everything," it means "cover the relevant space completely"

## Data Narration Protocol

When the brief provides data, metrics, or findings, follow this protocol to turn numbers into narrative.

### Step 1 — Contextualize

Never present a number in isolation. Always compare to something:

| Data type | Contextualize with |
|---|---|
| Revenue / growth | Prior period, forecast, competitor benchmark, market average |
| Customer metrics (NPS, CSAT, retention) | Industry benchmark, prior measurement, target |
| Cost / spend | Budget, prior period, percentage of revenue, competitor benchmarks |
| Market size | Adjacent markets, growth rate, penetration rate |
| Time / speed | Prior period, industry standard, competitor |

**Good:** "Revenue grew 23% year-over-year, outpacing the industry average of 12% and exceeding the company's own forecast of 18%."

**Bad:** "Revenue was $4.2M."

### Step 2 — Interpret

State what the number means for the reader's decision:

- "This retention rate suggests the product has achieved product-market fit in the mid-market segment."
- "At current burn rate, the company has 14 months of runway — sufficient to reach the next funding milestone if growth targets are met."
- "The 3:1 ratio of inbound to outbound leads indicates the brand has reached a level of market awareness that reduces customer acquisition cost over time."

### Step 3 — Caveat honestly

If data has limitations, say so:

- "This figure covers Q1-Q3 only; Q4 data was not available at time of writing."
- "Customer satisfaction scores are self-reported and may overstate actual sentiment."
- "The competitor benchmark is based on publicly available data, which may not reflect actual performance."

### Step 4 — Visualize decisions

When data supports a decision between options, present it as a comparison:

```
| Metric | Option A | Option B | Option C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue impact (Year 1) | +$2.1M | +$1.4M | +$3.8M |
| Implementation cost | $400K | $200K | $1.2M |
| Time to value | 6 months | 3 months | 12 months |
| Risk level | Medium | Low | High |
```

Tables force clarity. If you can't fill in a cell, you've found a data gap.

## Transition Protocol

Sections must flow as one continuous argument, not disconnected blocks.

### Between-section transitions

**Method 1 — Forward reference:** End section N with a sentence that sets up section N+1.
"With the competitive landscape mapped, the question becomes which positioning strategy best exploits the gaps identified above."

**Method 2 — Callback:** Start section N+1 by referencing a key finding from section N.
"The retention data presented above suggests a strong foundation — but retention alone doesn't explain the declining growth rate."

**Method 3 — Contrast:** Start section N+1 by contrasting with section N.
"While the financial analysis paints an optimistic picture, operational realities introduce constraints that temper those projections."

### Forbidden transitions

- "In this section, we will discuss..." — never announce what you're about to say, just say it
- "As mentioned above..." — vague back-references. Be specific about what was mentioned.
- "Moving on to..." — reads like a slide deck, not a document
- "It is also important to note that..." — if it's important, just state it directly

## Executive Summary Construction

The executive summary is written LAST, after all body sections are complete.

### Protocol

1. **Extract the core recommendation** from the Resolution / conclusion of the document
2. **Identify the 2-3 key supporting points** that are most compelling to the primary reader
3. **State the stakes** — what happens if the reader acts vs. doesn't act
4. **Keep to the specified length** — typically 2-3 paragraphs for consulting documents
5. **Test standalone quality** — read only the executive summary. Can the reader make a decision from this alone? If not, it's missing something.

### Rules

- Never introduce information in the summary that isn't in the body
- Never use weaker language in the summary than in the body — the summary should be the strongest version of the argument
- Lead with the recommendation, not the background
- If the document presents options, the summary must state which one is recommended and why
