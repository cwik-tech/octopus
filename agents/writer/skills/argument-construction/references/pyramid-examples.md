# Pyramid Principle Examples — Reference

Annotated examples of the Pyramid Principle (Minto) applied in executive business consulting documents. The core rule: lead with the answer, then support it.

---

## The Basic Structure

```
GOVERNING THOUGHT (the answer)
├── Key line 1 (main supporting argument)
│   ├── Supporting data / analysis
│   └── Supporting data / analysis
├── Key line 2 (main supporting argument)
│   ├── Supporting data / analysis
│   └── Supporting data / analysis
└── Key line 3 (main supporting argument)
    ├── Supporting data / analysis
    └── Supporting data / analysis
```

Every level answers the "why?" or "how do you know?" of the level above it.

---

## Example 1 — Executive Recommendation (Full Document)

**Governing thought (executive summary lead sentence):**
> Meridian should acquire TechFlow Ltd at the proposed £28M valuation. The acquisition accelerates product roadmap delivery by 18 months, adds a proven enterprise sales capability the company currently lacks, and is immediately accretive to gross margin.

**Key line 1:** Accelerates product roadmap by 18 months
- TechFlow's integration layer solves the three API (Application Programming Interface) connectivity gaps blocking Meridian's enterprise tier launch
- Internal build estimate was 14 months at £2.1M; TechFlow delivers this capability now
- Competitive window is closing: two competitors are expected to close similar feature gaps within 12 months

**Key line 2:** Adds proven enterprise sales capability
- TechFlow's 6-person enterprise team has closed 23 contracts above £200K in 18 months
- Average deal cycle of 4.2 months vs. Meridian's current 9.1 months in enterprise pilots
- Customer base includes 4 of Meridian's top 10 target accounts — immediate relationship transfer

**Key line 3:** Immediately accretive to gross margin
- TechFlow's gross margin is 74% vs. Meridian's blended 61%
- Combined entity projected at 67% gross margin in Year 1, 71% by Year 3
- £28M acquisition price represents 4.2x ARR — below comparable transactions averaging 5.8x

## Example 2 — Section-Level Pyramid (inside a larger document)

**Section heading:** Why the current pricing model is unsustainable

**Governing thought (section opening):**
> The current per-seat pricing model will cap revenue growth within 18 months and is already creating churn pressure in the enterprise segment.

**Supporting argument 1:** Per-seat model misaligns with enterprise value delivery
- Enterprise customers derive value from outcomes, not user counts — high-value customers are capped at lower prices than their usage warrants
- Three of the five churned enterprise accounts cited "pricing doesn't reflect the value we get" in exit interviews

**Supporting argument 2:** Competitive landscape has moved to usage-based pricing
- Four of the six direct competitors now offer usage-based or outcome-based pricing
- Two enterprise prospects cited competitor pricing models as a factor in their evaluation

**Supporting argument 3:** Revenue ceiling is approaching
- Current model caps Annual Contract Value (ACV) at £48K for a 200-seat enterprise (the maximum practical seat count)
- Usage-based modelling suggests these same accounts would generate £85-120K ACV under a consumption model
- The 23 enterprise accounts currently at the 200-seat cap represent £1.1M of constrained revenue

## Deductive vs. Inductive Grouping

Within each key line, arguments can be grouped two ways:

**Deductive (if-then logic):** Each point follows from the previous.
> "The market is growing faster than competitors are growing (1). Acme is positioned to capture disproportionate share (2). Therefore, Acme should accelerate investment now (3)."

**Inductive (parallel independent reasons):** Each point independently supports the same conclusion.
> "We should expand into Germany because: (1) it's our largest addressable market, (2) we have existing relationships there, (3) regulatory barriers are lower than other EU markets."

Use inductive grouping when each point stands independently. Use deductive when the logic is sequential. Never mix them in the same group without signalling the shift.

---

## Writing the Governing Thought

The governing thought is the hardest sentence in the document. It must:

1. Answer the question the document was written to address
2. Be specific enough to be falsifiable — "we should invest in growth" is not a governing thought
3. Contain the "so what" — not just what to do, but why

**Weak governing thought:** "This report analyses the options for international expansion and presents a recommendation."

**Strong governing thought:** "Acme should enter the German market in Q1 2026, prioritising the DACH (Germany, Austria, Switzerland) mid-market segment, with an initial investment of £1.2M expected to reach payback within 14 months."

The strong version is specific, actionable, and answers the key question immediately.
