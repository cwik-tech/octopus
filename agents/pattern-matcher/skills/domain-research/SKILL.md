# Domain Research Skill

Use this protocol to acquire domain expertise rapidly when facing unfamiliar industries, markets, or technical fields. Follow the four-step structure below, then synthesize findings into a domain brief for other agents.

## The Four-Step Protocol

### Step 1: Core Vocabulary, Key Players, and Regulatory Landscape

Build foundational knowledge first:

- **Identify core vocabulary.** Find 8-12 domain-specific terms that appear repeatedly in authoritative sources. Include at least one term you'd have guessed wrong without research (e.g., "pharming" in agriculture, "basis points" in finance).
- **Map key players.** Identify 5-10 organizations that define the domain: market leaders, regulators, standard-setters, advocacy groups. Note their role and influence.
- **Scan regulatory environment.** Find the primary regulator(s) for this domain. Understand what they regulate (products, practices, disclosures) and how recently rules changed.
- **Document three recent shifts.** What's changed in the last 2-3 years? New regulations, technology adoption, consolidation, market growth?

### Step 2: Value Chain Mapping

Understand how money and value flow:

- **Identify production layers.** Who makes raw inputs? Who processes them? Who distributes? Who consumes?
- **Find the money flows.** In which layer do margins concentrate? Where is competition intense vs. protected?
- **Locate switching costs.** What locks customers, suppliers, or partners into relationships? What would make them switch?
- **Spot dependencies.** Which players depend on others for survival? Where are bottlenecks?

### Step 3: Domain-Specific Constraints, Risks, and Failure Modes

Expose what could go wrong:

- **Technical constraints.** What physical, computational, or chemical limits exist? What's theoretically impossible?
- **Regulatory hazards.** Where do organizations stumble with compliance? What penalties apply?
- **Market risks.** Cyclicality? Substitution threats? Disintermediation? Commoditization?
- **Operational failure modes.** What breaks first under stress? What requires perfect execution?
- **Black swan factors.** What low-probability, high-impact event would reshape the domain? (Pandemic, regulation, competitor innovation.)

### Step 4: Analogies to Known Domains

Build intuition through comparison:

- **Find structural parallels.** What aspect resembles a domain you understand? (E.g., "cloud infrastructure is like electrical grids—commoditized, always-on, regulated by geography.")
- **Identify differences.** Where does the analogy break? What's fundamentally different?
- **Use analogies carefully.** Flag them as intuition-building, not proof.

### Step 5: Competitive Landscape (Optional but Recommended)

When the domain involves markets with multiple players, add competitive intelligence:

- **Map the competitive set.** Direct, adjacent, and substitute competitors — 3-5 per tier.
- **Decode positioning and strategy.** Value propositions, pricing models, target segments, recent strategic moves.
- **Analyze dynamics.** Market concentration, switching costs, network effects, convergence vs. divergence.
- **Identify whitespace.** Underserved segments, overserved segments, structural vulnerabilities, timing opportunities.

See `references/competitive-intelligence.md` for the full protocol.

This step is optional for purely technical or regulatory domains but recommended for any domain where commercial competition shapes outcomes.

## Source Evaluation Criteria

Assess credibility, recency, and relevance:

- **Primary vs. secondary.** Primary sources (regulatory filings, earnings calls, original research) carry more weight. Secondary sources (news, analysis) are faster but risk distortion.
- **Recency.** Prefer sources published in the last 1-2 years. Flag older sources as potentially outdated.
- **Vendor bias.** Discount marketing materials and vendor whitepapers by 50%. They're biased toward selling.
- **Regulatory authority.** Government bodies and industry standard-setters are highly credible on their domain.
- **Triangulation.** When three independent sources agree, confidence rises. Conflict signals nuance or bias.
- **Author credentials.** Does the author work in or study the domain? Or parachute in from unrelated fields?

See `references/source-evaluation.md` for detailed guidance.

## Synthesis: The Domain Brief

Produce a consumable brief for other agents:

- Distill findings into a domain overview (2-3 sentences).
- List core vocabulary with definitions.
- Draw a value chain diagram or text description.
- Name major players and their roles.
- State regulatory constraints.
- List domain-specific risks and failure modes.
- Include structural analogies.
- Rate confidence (high/medium/low) for each claim.
- Cite sources.

Use the template at `templates/domain-brief.md`.

## Anti-Patterns: What Isn't Real Domain Knowledge

Avoid these traps:

- **Surface jargon.** Using domain terms without understanding what they mean. ("The API leverages blockchain synergies.") Test yourself: can you explain it to a skeptic?
- **Outdated assumptions.** Treating five-year-old market structures as current. Always check recency.
- **Vendor marketing as fact.** "Enterprise AI adoption is skyrocketing" may just be a vendor's marketing narrative, not market reality.
- **Overgeneralization.** Assuming all players in a domain follow the same model. Exceptions often matter most.
- **Neglecting regulation.** Missing regulatory context is a critical gap. Always ask: "Who regulates this, and what are they watching?"

## Quick Start

1. Spend 10 minutes identifying vocabulary and key players (Step 1).
2. Spend 15 minutes mapping the value chain (Step 2).
3. Spend 10 minutes identifying risks and constraints (Step 3).
4. Spend 5 minutes sketching analogies (Step 4).
5. (Optional) Spend 15 minutes mapping competitive landscape (Step 5).
6. Synthesize into a domain brief using the template.

Total time: ~45 minutes for a working domain model (~60 minutes with competitive landscape).
