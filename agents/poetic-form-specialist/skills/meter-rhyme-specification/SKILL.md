# Meter and Rhyme Specification

A form name is not a specification. *Quatrains* could mean a dozen things. This skill produces a template that a poet can draft against and a critic can check against.

## 1. Specify Meter or Line Length

Choose one of three levels of strictness and say which one applies:

- **Strict meter:** name the foot and the count, for example iambic tetrameter. Allow one substitution per line, such as a trochaic opening. Use this for formal sonnets, heroic couplets, and ceremonial pieces delivered by confident readers.
- **Accentual or loose meter:** set a count of stresses per line, for example 4 stresses with any number of unstressed syllables. Use this for most spoken toasts and ballads, because it sounds natural in untrained mouths.
- **Syllable range:** for example 7 to 10 syllables per line, with no metrical pattern required. Use this for free verse that must still stay speakable.

Write one illustrative scansion pattern, such as `da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM`, rather than sample content.

## 2. Issue the Rhyme Verdict

Every specification states one of the following verdicts:

| Verdict | Use when |
|---------|----------|
| **Full rhyme** | The occasion wants lift, wit, ritual, or memorability: toasts, birthdays, roasts, and light anniversaries |
| **Slant rhyme** | The occasion wants warmth without jingle: weddings, tender anniversaries, gentle elegies, and details that resist perfect rhyme |
| **Unrhymed** | The occasion needs intimacy or grief, the details are dense or unrhymable, or rhyme would force the poet to trade truth for sound |

Always give a one-line reason. Test the verdict against the three hardest must-include words. If they have no usable rhymes, downgrade the verdict to slant rhyme or to unrhymed, or move those words to mid-line positions.

## 3. Specify the Rhyme Scheme and Type

- Use letter notation: AABB, ABAB, ABCB, ABBA, or AA couplets.
- **ABCB** is the most forgiving for untrained writers and readers, because only half the lines must rhyme.
- **AABB** hits hard and suits comedy, but tires quickly after about 16 lines.
- **Masculine rhyme** falls on a stressed final syllable, as in *day / way*. It is strong, clear, and best for spoken endings.
- **Feminine rhyme** falls on a stressed syllable followed by an unstressed one, as in *laughter / after*. It is softer and more wistful, and risks comedy when overused.
- Rhymes should land on concrete, meaningful words, never on filler such as *so*, *too*, or *indeed*.

## 4. Specify Stanza Shape and Count

- Stanza length: couplet, tercet, quatrain, sestet, octave, or open form
- Stanza count, derived from the line budget
- Whether stanzas are regular or may vary
- The turn: where the poem shifts, for example from memory to blessing or from lament to consolation

## 5. Specify Refrains and Repeated Elements

- The position of each refrain line or anaphoric opening
- Whether the refrain may vary slightly on each return
- Confirmation that the sensitivity notes cleared the repeated content

## 6. Mark Fixed and Free Elements

Separate the constraints the poet must not break from the choices the poet may vary. Typical fixed elements are the length budget, the rhyme verdict, the refrain, and an acrostic spine. Typical free elements are word choice, image order, and occasional metrical substitution.

## Anti-Patterns to Prevent

- **Forced inversion:** *in love we fell* to catch a rhyme. Specify slant rhyme instead.
- **Filler padding:** *oh so very* used to hit a syllable count. Widen the syllable range.
- **Sing-song in grief:** regular tetrameter with perfect rhymes in a eulogy. Loosen the meter or drop the rhyme.
- **Monotony:** thirty lines of AABB. Break the pattern with a stanza shift or a closing turn.
- **Name mangling:** a real name forced into the wrong stress. Place names where their natural stress fits the line.
