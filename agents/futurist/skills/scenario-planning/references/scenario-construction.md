# Scenario Construction: The 2×2 Matrix Method

## Building Good Scenarios

### Selecting Uncertainties

The quality of scenarios depends entirely on choosing the right two uncertainties. Good uncertainties:

1. **Are genuinely uncertain.** Experts disagree on the outcome. If 90% of experts agree, it's a trend — build it into ALL scenarios as a given.
2. **Have high impact.** The difference between Pole A and Pole B fundamentally changes the strategic landscape.
3. **Are largely independent.** If Uncertainty 1 going to Pole A makes Uncertainty 2 very likely to go to Pole A, they're correlated and will produce only 2 distinct scenarios.
4. **Are relevant to the focal question.** The uncertainty matters for the specific strategic decision at hand.

### Testing Your Uncertainty Selection

Ask these questions:
- "Can I argue persuasively for both poles?" If one pole seems implausible, the uncertainty isn't genuine.
- "If I knew which pole would materialize, would it change my strategy?" If not, the uncertainty isn't high-impact.
- "Are these two uncertainties telling the same story?" If yes, they're correlated. Find a more independent second axis.

### Naming Scenarios

Good scenario names are:
- **Memorable:** Short, evocative, easy to reference in conversation.
- **Neutral:** Don't signal which scenario is "good" or "bad." Every scenario has winners and losers.
- **Descriptive:** Hint at the scenario's defining characteristic.

Examples:
- "Digital Fortress" (high regulation + rapid tech adoption)
- "Wild West" (low regulation + rapid tech adoption)
- "Steady State" (high regulation + slow tech adoption)
- "Slow Burn" (low regulation + slow tech adoption)

Avoid: "Best Case," "Worst Case," "Most Likely," "Scenario 1."

### Writing Scenario Narratives

Each narrative should:
1. **Set the scene.** What does this world look like at the end of the time horizon? Paint a picture.
2. **Explain how we got here.** What events, decisions, and trends led to this outcome? Make the path plausible.
3. **Identify winners and losers.** Who thrives? Who struggles? Why?
4. **Surface implications for the focal question.** What does this world mean for our specific strategic decision?

**Length:** 1-2 paragraphs per scenario. Long enough to be vivid, short enough to be memorable.

### Worked Example: SaaS Company Entering Healthcare

**Focal question:** "Should we build a HIPAA-compliant healthcare vertical in the next 3 years?"

**Driving forces identified:**
- Healthcare digitization pace (accelerating vs. stalling)
- Regulatory complexity (increasing vs. simplifying)
- Big tech healthcare entry (aggressive vs. retreating)
- Payer consolidation (more concentrated vs. fragmented)

**Two critical uncertainties selected:**
- Axis 1: Healthcare digitization pace (fast vs. slow)
- Axis 2: Regulatory complexity (increasing vs. decreasing)

**Four scenarios:**

| | Fast Digitization | Slow Digitization |
|---|---|---|
| **Increasing Regulation** | "Compliance Gold Rush" — Massive demand for digital health tools, but regulatory requirements make development expensive and slow. Advantage: compliance-first players. | "Paper Fortress" — Healthcare resists digitization AND regulation tightens. Worst case for tech entrants. Incumbents with legacy relationships dominate. |
| **Decreasing Regulation** | "Digital Health Boom" — Rapid adoption, lower barriers. Best case for tech entrants. Land grab dynamics; speed matters more than compliance depth. | "Status Quo Plus" — Regulation eases but healthcare doesn't digitize faster. Incremental opportunity. Patience required; ROI timeline extends. |

**Strategy testing:**
- "Build the vertical" succeeds in "Compliance Gold Rush" and "Digital Health Boom" but struggles in "Paper Fortress."
- "Partner with an incumbent" is robust across all scenarios but limits upside in "Digital Health Boom."
- "Wait and monitor" avoids downside but misses the first-mover advantage in "Compliance Gold Rush."

## Quality Checks for Scenarios

### Internal Consistency
Each scenario must be internally coherent. If you claim "fast digitization" but then describe hospitals still using fax machines, the narrative contradicts itself.

### Plausibility
Every scenario should be plausible — a reasonable person could argue it might happen. If a scenario requires multiple unlikely events to coincide, it's not plausible enough.

### Distinctiveness
The four scenarios should feel genuinely different. If Scenario 1 and Scenario 2 lead to similar strategic implications, your uncertainties may be correlated or low-impact.

### Completeness
Together, the four scenarios should cover the range of plausible futures. If there's an obvious "what if X?" that none of the scenarios captures, consider whether your uncertainty axes are correct.

### Actionability
Each scenario should lead to different strategic implications. If the same strategy works in all four scenarios, either the strategy is genuinely robust (good!) or the scenarios aren't different enough (problem!).
