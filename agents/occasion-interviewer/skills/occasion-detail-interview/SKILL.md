# Occasion Detail Interview

## Goal

Collect enough true, specific material for a poem that could only be about this person and this moment, while asking as few questions as possible.

## Essentials for every occasion

Drafting is blocked until these are known:

1. **Honoree names:** full name, the name people actually use, and pronunciation for anything that is not obvious.
2. **Speaker and relationship:** who reads the poem aloud and how they relate to the honoree.
3. **Audience:** who will be in the room, roughly how many people, and how well they know the honoree.
4. **Three specifics that pass the greeting-card test:** details a stranger could not have written.
5. **The sensitivity check:** see the `sensitivity-screening` skill.

## Asking for scenes, not adjectives

- If the requester says 'He is generous', ask: 'Tell me about a time he gave something away that surprised you.'
- If the requester says 'They are perfect together', ask: 'What is a small everyday thing they do for each other that others might not notice?'
- If the requester says 'She loved her garden', ask: 'What did she grow, and what did she do with it?'

Always probe for a **place**, an **object**, a **sound or smell**, and a **line someone actually said**.

## Question bank by occasion

**Wedding or anniversary**
- How did they meet, and what is the version of that story they tell?
- What is a ritual or habit that belongs only to them?
- What does each one call the other?
- What was the moment you knew this was serious?
- For anniversaries: which milestone year is it, and what have they built or survived together?

**Eulogy or memorial**
- Open by asking permission: 'Is it all right if I ask a few questions about her?'
- What did a normal Saturday with them look like?
- What phrase or saying will everyone recognize as theirs?
- What did their hands do, such as cooking, fixing, or writing?
- Is there anything about how they died that the family wants left unsaid?

**Birthday, especially milestones**
- Which birthday is this, and how does the honoree feel about the number?
- What has changed in the last year or decade that they are proud of?
- What is the running joke among friends or family?

**Retirement**
- What was the work, in their own words, not the job title?
- What will colleagues miss, and what will they not miss?
- What are they retiring to?

**Toast**
- How long does the speaker have, and how formal is the room?
- One story that will make the room laugh, and one line that will make them raise a glass.

**Gift poem**
- What is the gift or occasion it accompanies?
- Will this be read privately or aloud?

## Prioritizing a round

- Seven questions or fewer per round, ordered by importance.
- Tag each question [Essential] or [Helpful].
- Combine related asks into a single question where you can.
- For eulogies, ask no more than five questions and lead with care.
- If the brief already answers a question, do not ask it again. Confirm it instead, but only if it is ambiguous.

## Stopping rule

Stop interviewing once the essentials are covered and you have one Anchor-quality story. More material has diminishing returns. The poet needs a few strong specifics, not an exhaustive biography.
