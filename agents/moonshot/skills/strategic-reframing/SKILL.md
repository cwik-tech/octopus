# Strategic Reframing Skill

Every other skill in this toolkit accepts the problem as given and works within that frame. This skill challenges the frame itself. Use it when incremental improvement isn't enough — when the question isn't "how do we do this better?" but "should we be doing this at all?"

The distinction matters: Creative Ideation generates novel solutions to a stated problem. Decision Analysis evaluates options within a defined space. Strategic Reframing asks whether the problem statement, the market definition, or the competitive frame is itself the constraint — and reconstructs it.

---

## When to Use This Skill

- The competitive landscape is crowded and differentiation is shrinking
- The stated problem assumes the current market structure is fixed
- Stakeholders keep reaching the same conclusions regardless of the analysis
- The client or team says "we need a breakthrough, not an improvement"
- Problem Decomposition reveals that all sub-problems trace back to a structural constraint in the market or business model
- The domain research shows convergence — all players competing on the same factors
- An engagement calls for transformative rather than incremental strategy

## When NOT to Use This Skill

- The problem is tactical or operational ("fix this bug," "reduce costs 10%")
- The problem is diagnostic ("why did revenue drop?")
- Stakeholders have explicitly scoped the engagement as incremental improvement
- Time pressure demands a solution within the current frame — reframing takes time
- The current frame is working well and the goal is optimization, not transformation

## Strategic Reframing Protocol

### Step 1: Diagnose the Current Frame (10-15 minutes)

Before challenging the frame, understand it precisely:

1. **State the implicit frame.** What problem is being solved? What market is being competed in? What customer is being served? What success looks like? Often, the frame is unstated — surface it explicitly.
   - "The implicit frame is: we are a [type of company] competing in [market] for [customer segment], and winning means [metric]."

2. **Map the competitive factors.** What dimensions do all competitors in this space compete on? Price, features, speed, quality, service, brand? This is the Strategy Canvas from Blue Ocean Strategy — see `references/blue-ocean-strategy.md`.

3. **Identify the "category orthodoxy."** What does everyone in this category believe is true? What assumptions are so deeply shared that no one questions them?
   - "Everyone in this space assumes that [X], that customers want [Y], and that the business model must involve [Z]."

4. **Find the "stuck point."** Where does every analysis of this problem converge on the same conclusion? That convergence point is where the frame is constraining thinking.

### Step 2: Apply Reframing Lenses (20-30 minutes)

Work through these lenses systematically. Not all will produce insights for every problem — but each forces a different angle of attack on the current frame.

#### Lens 1: Blue Ocean — Reconstruct Market Boundaries

Use the Six Paths Framework to look across boundaries the industry takes for granted:

1. **Look across alternative industries.** Not just competitors — alternatives. What do customers do *instead* of using your category? (People don't just choose between CRM (Customer Relationship Management) systems — they choose between CRM systems, spreadsheets, notebooks, and memory.)
2. **Look across strategic groups.** What if you combined the advantages of the premium tier with the accessibility of the low-end tier?
3. **Look across the buyer chain.** Who actually uses, pays for, and influences the purchase? What if you targeted a different member of the chain?
4. **Look across complementary offerings.** What happens before, during, and after someone uses your product? What pain exists in the total experience, not just your slice?
5. **Look across functional-emotional orientation.** If your industry competes on function, what if you competed on emotion (or vice versa)?
6. **Look across time.** What trend, if it continues, will make the current category obsolete? How would you design for that future?

Apply the Four Actions Framework to each insight: what factors can you **Eliminate** (that the industry takes for granted), **Reduce** (well below standard), **Raise** (well above standard), or **Create** (that the industry has never offered)?

See `references/blue-ocean-strategy.md` for the full protocol, Strategy Canvas construction, and worked examples.

#### Lens 2: Category Design — Define a New Game

Instead of competing in an existing category, ask whether you should design and own a new one:

1. **Identify the category's "missing."** What problem does everyone in the category accept as unsolvable or not their job? The "missing" is the gap between what the category provides and what people actually need.
2. **Define the new category.** Give it a name. What is it? Who is it for? What does it replace? A category isn't a product — it's a frame of reference that makes your product the obvious choice.
3. **Develop a Point of View (POV).** Articulate why the old category is broken, what the new category is, and why now. The POV is the argument for why the world needs a new frame — it redefines the problem, not just the solution.
4. **Identify the category king dynamics.** In most new categories, one player captures 70-80% of the value. What would it take to be that player?

See `references/category-design.md` for the full methodology.

#### Lens 3: Zero-to-One — Find the Secret

Peter Thiel's framework for identifying opportunities everyone else is missing:

1. **The Contrarian Question:** "What important truth do very few people agree with you on?" Applied to the problem: what does this team believe about the market that almost no one else does?
2. **The Monopoly Question:** "Are you starting with a big share of a small market?" If not, can you redefine the market to make that true?
3. **The Secret:** What's something true about this domain that most participants don't know or refuse to acknowledge? Secrets hide in plain sight — in customer complaints everyone ignores, in trends everyone dismisses, in assumptions no one tests.
4. **Definite Optimism:** Don't build for a vague better future. Define specifically what the future should look like and build backward from there.

#### Lens 4: 10x Constraint — Force Structural Rethinking

Apply a forcing function: "What if we had to achieve 10x the result, not 10% more?"

- 10% improvement invites optimization within the current frame.
- 10x improvement makes optimization impossible — you *must* rethink the approach.

Ask: "What would a 10x version of this look like?" Not 10x effort — 10x *outcome*. If the answer is "that's impossible with the current approach," good. That's the point. Now ask: "What approach *would* make it possible?"

Common 10x moves:
- Eliminate the human bottleneck entirely (automation, self-service)
- Change who the customer is (serve the underserved, skip the obvious segment)
- Change the business model (from selling products to selling outcomes)
- Change the delivery mechanism (from high-touch to zero-touch, from physical to digital)
- Change the unit of value (from per-seat to per-outcome, from subscription to transaction)

#### Lens 5: Disruptive Innovation — Find the Asymmetric Opportunity

Clayton Christensen's framework identifies where incumbents *can't* respond:

1. **Low-end disruption:** Is there a segment being overserved? Customers paying for features they don't need? A simpler, cheaper version could capture the bottom of the market, then move up.
2. **New-market disruption:** Are there non-consumers — people who don't use any solution today because existing options are too expensive, complex, or inaccessible? Build for them.
3. **The Asymmetric Motivation Test:** Will the incumbent be *motivated* to respond? If your approach targets their least profitable customers or requires cannibalizing their existing business, they probably won't — even if they could.

See `references/disruptive-innovation.md` for the full framework and worked examples.

### Step 3: Evaluate Reframes (15 minutes)

Not every reframe is viable. For each reframe that emerged from Step 2:

1. **Articulate the reframe.** "Instead of [old frame], we should think of this as [new frame]."
2. **The "So What?" Test.** If we adopted this frame, what would we do differently? If the answer is "nothing much," the reframe is cosmetic, not structural.
3. **The "Who Cares?" Test.** Who benefits from this reframe? Is there a real customer with a real need who would prefer the reframed approach? If it's intellectually interesting but no one would pay for it, it's academic.
4. **The "Why Now?" Test.** What has changed (technology, regulation, culture, economics) that makes this reframe viable now when it wasn't before? If nothing has changed, someone probably tried it already.
5. **The "Why Not?" Test.** If this reframe is so compelling, why hasn't someone done it? The answer might be "no one thought of it" (good). Or it might be "it's structurally impossible" (bad). Or "someone tried and failed" (investigate why).
6. **Magnitude of shift.** Rate each reframe:
   - **Incremental reframe:** Same market, different positioning. (Useful but not transformative.)
   - **Adjacent reframe:** New market segment or business model. (Significant shift, manageable risk.)
   - **Category reframe:** New category entirely. (Transformative, highest risk and reward.)

### Step 4: Develop the Reframing Brief (15 minutes)

For the 1-2 most promising reframes, produce a structured brief:

1. **The old frame** — what everyone currently assumes.
2. **The new frame** — the reframed problem/market/category.
3. **The evidence** — what supports this reframe (market signals, non-customer analysis, trend data, analogies).
4. **The strategic move** — what you would do differently under the new frame.
5. **The risk** — what could make this reframe wrong.
6. **The test** — the smallest experiment that would validate or invalidate the reframe.

Use the template at `templates/reframing-brief.md`.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Reframing as procrastination.** "We need to rethink the whole approach" can be a way to avoid doing the work. Reframing should produce a new *direction*, not an excuse for inaction.
- **Cosmetic reframing.** Renaming the problem without changing what you'd do about it. If the reframe doesn't change the strategy, it's not a reframe.
- **Reframing without evidence.** "What if we created a whole new category?" needs supporting evidence — market signals, non-customer demand, technology enablers. Without evidence, it's wishful thinking.
- **Ignoring the current frame's strengths.** The current frame exists for reasons. It captured real value. Acknowledge what works before discarding it.
- **Reframing everything.** Not every problem needs reframing. Most operational and diagnostic problems are correctly framed. Reserve this skill for strategic and generative problems where the frame itself might be the constraint.
- **Falling in love with the reframe.** A beautiful reframe that fails the "Who Cares?" and "Why Now?" tests is an intellectual exercise, not a strategy.

## Integration with Other Skills

- **Domain Research** provides the market context, competitive landscape, and trend data that feed into reframing. The competitive intelligence reference is especially relevant — convergence patterns (all players competing on the same factors) are a strong signal that reframing is needed.
- **Creative Ideation** generates solutions *within* a frame; Strategic Reframing challenges the frame itself. Use reframing first to set the right frame, then ideation to generate solutions within it.
- **Problem Decomposition** may classify a problem as Strategic or Generative, which can trigger reframing. A reframe may also change the decomposition entirely — what looked like 5 sub-problems under the old frame might collapse to 2 under the new frame.
- **Decision Analysis** evaluates reframed options against criteria. The reframing brief feeds directly into Decision Analysis as an option.
- **Scenario Planning** and Strategic Reframing complement each other: scenarios test whether a reframe holds across multiple futures, and scenario narratives can inspire reframes ("in this future, the current category doesn't exist — what replaces it?").
- **Argument Synthesis** builds the case for the reframe. A reframe needs a strong POV (Point of View) — the Argument Construction Protocol (Claim-Evidence-Reasoning-Implications) is the right structure for presenting it.
