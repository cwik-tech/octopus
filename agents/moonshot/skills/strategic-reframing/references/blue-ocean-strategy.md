# Blue Ocean Strategy: Reconstructing Market Boundaries

## Core Concept

Red oceans are existing market spaces where competitors fight for share of existing demand. Blue oceans are new market spaces where demand is created, not fought over. The goal isn't to outperform competitors — it's to make competition irrelevant by changing the playing field.

## The Strategy Canvas

A Strategy Canvas maps the competitive factors an industry competes on and shows how different players invest across those factors.

### How to Build One

1. **Identify the industry's competitive factors.** What do buyers receive and compare when choosing among offerings? Typically 5-12 factors. (Price, features, brand, service, speed, customization, reliability, etc.)
2. **Plot competitors.** For each factor, rate the industry's level of offering (low to high). Plot major competitors as lines on the canvas.
3. **Find the convergence.** If all competitors' lines look similar, the industry is competing on the same factors at similar levels — this is a red ocean. The more the lines converge, the stronger the case for reframing.
4. **Draw a divergent value curve.** A blue ocean strategy creates a value curve that looks *different* from the pack — high where they're low, low where they're high, and offering factors they don't.

### Worked Example: Cirque du Soleil vs. Traditional Circus

Traditional circus factors: Star performers, Animal acts, Aisle concessions, Multiple show arenas, Fun and humor, Thrill and danger.

Cirque du Soleil value curve:
- **Eliminated:** Star performers, Animal acts, Aisle concessions, Multiple arenas
- **Reduced:** Fun and humor, Thrill and danger
- **Raised:** Unique venue experience
- **Created:** Theme, Artistic music and dance, Refined environment, Multiple productions

Result: Didn't compete with traditional circuses. Created a new market space between circus and theater. Charged 5-10x traditional circus prices. Made the competition (Ringling Bros.) irrelevant.

## The Six Paths Framework

Six systematic ways to look across industry boundaries:

### Path 1: Look Across Alternative Industries

Not substitute products — alternative *ways customers solve the same problem*.

Ask: "What do customers do instead of using our category?" and "Why do they choose that alternative?"

Example: A financial planning firm doesn't just compete with other financial planners. Alternatives include: doing nothing (hope for the best), using free online tools, asking friends, reading books, hiring an accountant. Each alternative reveals something about what customers actually value vs. what the industry assumes they value.

### Path 2: Look Across Strategic Groups

Strategic groups are sets of companies within an industry that pursue similar strategies (luxury vs. budget, full-service vs. self-service).

Ask: "What if you combined the advantages of the high-end strategic group with the accessibility of the low-end group?"

Example: Lexus offered near-Mercedes quality at Cadillac prices. The industry assumed luxury required luxury pricing. It didn't.

### Path 3: Look Across the Buyer Chain

In most industries, there are users (who use the product), purchasers (who pay), and influencers (who shape the decision). Industries often focus on one.

Ask: "What if we targeted a different member of the buyer chain than our competitors?"

Example: Enterprise software traditionally sold to CIOs (Chief Information Officers) (purchasers). Slack targeted individual teams (users), who then pressured CIOs to buy.

### Path 4: Look Across Complementary Offerings

What happens before, during, and after someone uses your product? The total experience often has pain points the industry ignores because they're "not our product."

Ask: "What's the biggest hassle customers experience in the total journey, not just our product?"

Example: IKEA (Ingka Holding AB) didn't just sell furniture — they designed for the total experience: browsing (showroom), deciding (full room setups), transporting (flat-pack), assembling (instructions + tools), and even eating (in-store restaurant). Competitors sold furniture and stopped there.

### Path 5: Look Across Functional-Emotional Orientation

Some industries compete on function (price, features, utility). Others compete on emotion (brand, status, experience).

Ask: "If our industry competes on function, what if we competed on emotion?" (Or vice versa.)

Example: Swatch turned watches (functional, commodity) into fashion accessories (emotional, collectible). Same manufacturing cost. Completely different value and pricing.

### Path 6: Look Across Time

Identify trends that are clearly developing and ask: "What would our industry look like if this trend reaches its logical conclusion?"

Ask: "What trend, if extrapolated 5-10 years, would make our current category obsolete? How would we design for that future now?"

## The Four Actions Framework

For each factor on the Strategy Canvas:

- **Eliminate:** Which factors that the industry takes for granted should be eliminated entirely?
- **Reduce:** Which factors should be reduced well below the industry standard?
- **Raise:** Which factors should be raised well above the industry standard?
- **Create:** Which factors should be created that the industry has never offered?

The power is in the combination. Eliminating and reducing frees resources. Raising and creating deploys them toward new value. The result is a value curve that diverges from competitors — not a more expensive product, but a differently valued one.

### Applying Four Actions

For each factor on your Strategy Canvas, ask:
- "If we eliminated this, would customers still come to us?" If yes — eliminate.
- "Are customers over-served on this dimension?" If yes — reduce.
- "Is there a dimension where all players are mediocre but customers care deeply?" If yes — raise.
- "Is there something customers need that no one in the category provides?" If yes — create.

## Three Tiers of Non-Customers

Most companies focus on existing customers. Blue ocean strategy focuses on *non-customers* — the much larger pool of people who don't use your category.

### Tier 1: Soon-to-Be Non-Customers

People at the edge of your market who use your category minimally and are ready to leave. They use it because they have no better option — not because they're satisfied.

Ask: "Who uses our category reluctantly? Why are they dissatisfied? What would make them leave?"

### Tier 2: Refusing Non-Customers

People who consciously chose *not* to use your category. They've considered it and decided against it.

Ask: "Who has looked at our category and said 'no thanks'? What turned them off? What would change their mind?"

### Tier 3: Unexplored Non-Customers

People in distant markets who have never considered your category as an option. They don't think your category is relevant to them.

Ask: "Who doesn't even know our category exists? If they did, what form would the offering need to take for them to care?"

## For AI Agents

- **Build the Strategy Canvas first.** It's the diagnostic tool that reveals whether reframing is needed. If all competitors' value curves converge, the industry is ripe for blue ocean strategy.
- **Prioritize non-customers over customers.** Existing customers anchor you to the current frame. Non-customers reveal where the new frame might be.
- **Use all six paths.** Don't stop at the first path that produces an insight. Each path reveals a different kind of opportunity.
- **The Four Actions Framework is the output tool.** After identifying the opportunity, formalize it as Eliminate-Reduce-Raise-Create. This makes the reframe concrete and actionable.
- **Test for blue ocean vs. red ocean thinking.** If the proposed strategy requires beating competitors on their own terms, it's still red ocean. A true blue ocean move makes the competition question irrelevant.
