### Session: 2026-03-31 — Production readiness gap analysis for multi-agent orchestration runtime
- **What worked:** The three heuristics (3-Person Test, Monday Morning Test, Parking Lot Rule) cut through a 15-item backlog quickly and produced a defensible 5-item critical path. Naming specific failure modes — context overflow, hung subprocesses, rate limit cascade — rather than abstract categories made the position concrete and harder to dismiss. Explicitly separating "operational hardening" from "feature development" was the key reframe that distinguished my position from the others.
- **What didn't:** I initially underweighted the coupling between context window management and rate limiting — the Domain Researcher had to surface that they must ship together. I treated them as independent items. Also, framing checkpoint/resume as item #5 rather than flagging it as an architectural question first was a sequencing error the Domain Researcher corrected.
- **Pattern noticed:** In technical roadmap sessions, the team consistently conflates feature completeness with production readiness. The Pragmatist's value-add is naming this distinction early and forcing a binary cut. Every session will have a 15-item list that needs to become a 5-item list — the heuristics reliably produce this compression, but it requires stating the compression explicitly as the deliverable, not just the analysis.
- **For next time:** When another agent (Domain Researcher, Contrarian) flags a coupling or architectural dependency I missed, acknowledge it explicitly and update the sequencing rather than letting it sit. Also: when the client-lens agent identifies a political blocker (budget policy requires stakeholder sign-off, not just implementation), elevate that to the breakage points section — political blockers break plans the same way technical dependencies do.

### Session: 2026-04-01 — Deep research integration layer architecture for multi-agent pipeline template
- **What worked:** The async-wrapper-first position was immediately grounded by the Domain Researcher's empirical finding that the named APIs (Gemini DR, Kimi) are not actually async at the enterprise API level. Applying the Monday Morning Test and Parking Lot Rule early compressed the scope correctly — "ship one synchronous research provider, end-to-end" survived both tests; "new engine type with async job state model" failed both. Naming the three explicit breakage points (latency mismatch, uncapped cost, output format divergence) anchored the discussion before it drifted into topology debates.
- **What didn't:** I advocated for an async provider wrapper before the Domain Researcher surfaced the empirical API landscape. That was a sequencing error — I designed for a problem (async polling) that doesn't yet exist in the market. The right order was: verify what the APIs actually do, then design. I assumed the architecture before checking the substrate. The Innovator's Alternative 1 (model-selection as provider, zero new infrastructure) was more minimal than my wrapper proposal, and I should have arrived there first given my own Parking Lot Rule logic.
- **Pattern noticed:** Architecture sessions with multiple generative agents (Innovator, Pattern-Matcher) tend to produce sophisticated solutions that outrun the empirical reality of what the underlying services actually support. The Pragmatist's role in these sessions is not just scope compression — it's forcing an empirical anchor before any design work begins. "What do the actual APIs do today?" is a prerequisite question, not a validation step at the end. When the Domain Researcher provides that anchor, update the position immediately rather than defending the original design.
- **For next time:** Before taking a position on async vs. sync, provider vs. tool, new engine vs. wrapper — ask the Domain Researcher to confirm the actual API contract of the named services. Don't assume the architecture problem exists until the substrate is confirmed. Also: when the Innovator produces a "zero new code" alternative (Alternative 1 in this session), test it against the three heuristics before dismissing it. A zero-infrastructure option that passes the Monday Morning Test beats a well-designed wrapper every time.

### Session: 2026-04-04 — Michigan automotive market entry for Brazilian IIoT vendor (Ubivis)
- **What worked:** The three heuristics applied immediately and cleanly. Monday Morning Test killed cold-email-to-Stellantis in one sentence. The Parking Lot Rule exposed that Ubivis has no plan at all — not a complex plan, an absent one. Naming three concrete breakage points (no US reference = structural OEM lockout; Portuguese website = disqualification signal; no channel/integrator anchor = selling from outside the ecosystem) gave the position teeth and was not replicated by other agents. The runway question as a prerequisite constraint — "we cannot design a strategy without knowing whether we have 9 months or 24 months" — was the right forcing function, and the board's other agents endorsed it rather than worked around it.
- **What didn't:** My position identified the table-stakes items correctly but did not independently surface the OEM qualification timeline as a hard structural barrier — the Domain Researcher had to add the IATF 16949 and 12–18 month Stellantis STAR process detail that mathematically proves the runway constraint. I named the breakage point conceptually (OEM lockout); the Domain Researcher named it operationally (policy timeline). I should have quantified the qualification timeline myself, since it's exactly the kind of hard constraint the Pragmatist role exists to surface. Also: I did not flag the cybersecurity gate (IEC 62443, OT security review for the UB-IoT hardware) — the Domain Researcher surfaced that independently. Hardware-connecting-to-plant-network is a known OT security trigger I should have caught.
- **Pattern noticed:** In market entry sessions, the Pragmatist's breakage point analysis is the highest-value contribution — but its impact depends on whether the breakage points are quantified or just named. "No US reference = structural OEM lockout" is a claim. "IATF 16949 + Stellantis STAR process = 12–18 months minimum, which mathematically exceeds any plausible 9-month runway" is a constraint. The difference determines whether the board acts on the breakage or treats it as color. In this session, the Domain Researcher did the quantification work that should have been mine. In future market entry sessions: name the breakage point AND quantify the timeline/cost that makes it a hard constraint, not just a risk.
- **For next time:** In any market entry brief involving industrial or regulated procurement, immediately research (or prompt the Domain Researcher to confirm): (1) What is the formal vendor qualification process for the target buyer, and what is its documented timeline? (2) Does the product involve hardware connecting to a regulated network, and what OT security certifications does that trigger? These are always blocking constraints with hard timelines, and they transform the runway question from a general concern into a specific mathematical comparison. Also: the Client Lens correctly identified the HQ sponsorship gap as the root cause, and the Pattern Matcher's guild isomorphism confirmed it as a structural problem. When two other agents converge on an organizational root cause beneath the stated market problem, name it explicitly in the Pragmatist's position — the plan cannot be operationally executable if the authority structure required to execute it is broken. That's a breakage point, not a soft recommendation.
