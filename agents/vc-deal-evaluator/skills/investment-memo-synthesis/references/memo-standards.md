# Investment Memo Standards

What separates a great investment memo from a mediocre one. Use this as a calibration reference when drafting or reviewing memos.

---

## Characteristics of a Great Memo

**Clarity of recommendation.** The reader knows what you think within 30 seconds. The recommendation is in the first paragraph, not buried at the end. "Invest $5M at $20M pre-money, with high conviction" — not "there are several factors to consider."

**Intellectual honesty.** The bear case is stated with the same rigor as the bull case. If you're recommending investment, the reader should still walk away understanding exactly why this might fail. Memos that only present positives are advocacy documents, not analysis.

**Evidence over narrative.** Every claim is tied to a specific data point. "Revenue is growing" is narrative. "ARR grew from $800K to $2.1M over the last 12 months, with 85% of growth from expansion revenue" is evidence. The best memos make you feel like you could verify every claim independently.

**Attribution and traceability.** Each conclusion can be traced to the analysis that produced it. "The market is large" came from the Market Analyst's bottom-up sizing. "The team can execute" came from the Founder Assessor's reference checks. This allows the committee to challenge specific reasoning chains rather than the entire memo.

**Tension preservation.** Real disagreements are presented, not smoothed over. "The Financial Modeler projects a $200M exit at current growth rates, but the Exit Strategist notes that comparable acquisitions in this sector averaged $80M. This tension hinges on whether the company's growth rate is sustainable." This gives the committee something to debate.

**Actionable risk register.** Risks are not a list of everything that could go wrong. They are the 3-5 specific things that matter most, each with a mitigation plan or monitoring trigger. "Technology risk exists" is useless. "The core algorithm has been validated in lab settings but not at production scale; we mitigate by structuring the investment with a milestone tranche tied to production deployment by Q3" is actionable.

---

## Common Memo Failures

**The advocacy memo.** Written to justify a decision already made. Tells: no bear case, no tensions, no risk register, every data point is positive. This is the most common failure and the most dangerous because it produces false confidence.

**The data dump.** Every fact about the company presented without hierarchy or judgment. The committee can't tell what matters. The market size is mentioned alongside the number of employees alongside the founder's college. No weighting, no synthesis.

**The hedged memo.** Every sentence qualified. "The market could be large." "The team seems strong." "There might be product-market fit." Hedging everywhere means the analyst either doesn't have an opinion or is afraid to state one. Both are problems.

**The narrative memo.** Reads like a magazine profile of the founder. Compelling story, emotional resonance, minimal data. Narrative fallacy at work — a great story substitutes for great evidence.

**The recency memo.** Over-weights the last quarter or the last meeting with the founder. Ignores longer trends, earlier data, and the base rate of outcomes for companies at this stage.

**The no-context memo.** States metrics without benchmarks. "$2M ARR" means nothing without knowing what good looks like at this stage. "120% NRR" means nothing without knowing the sector benchmark. Every metric needs context.

## Memo Structure Rules

1. **Lead with the answer.** Recommendation, conviction level, and one-sentence thesis — first paragraph.
2. **Pyramid structure.** Key supporting arguments at level 2. Evidence under each argument at level 3. Details only for the reader who wants to go deep.
3. **Separate bull case from bear case.** Distinct sections, each argued with full rigor.
4. **Risk register is a decision tool.** It should change how you structure the deal, not just acknowledge danger.
5. **End with what we don't know.** The most honest part of any memo is the open questions section. What would you want to know that you can't know yet?

---

## Calibration Notes

Track memo accuracy over time. For every deal you invest in, revisit the memo at 12, 24, and 36 months:

- Which bull case arguments proved correct? Which didn't?
- Which bear case arguments materialized? Which didn't?
- Which risks from the register actually happened?
- What killed the company (if applicable) — was it in the memo or a surprise?
- What was the biggest gap in the original analysis?

This creates a feedback loop that improves future memo quality. Without calibration, memo writing becomes ritual rather than analysis.
