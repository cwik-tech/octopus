# Cognitive Biases in Venture Capital

Twelve biases that systematically distort investment judgment. For each: the mechanism, how it manifests in VC, detection signals, and countermeasures.

---

## 1. FOMO (Fear of Missing Out)

**Mechanism:** Anxiety that others will capture an opportunity you'll regret missing. Urgency overrides analysis.

**VC manifestation:** "The round is closing Friday." "Three other funds have term sheets in." Compressed timelines that prevent thorough diligence. Investing because not investing feels more painful than losing capital.

**Detection signals:** You're moving faster than your standard process. You're skipping diligence steps. You feel physically anxious about missing the deal. The primary argument for investing is "we'll regret not being in this."

**Countermeasure:** Ask: "If this round closed and I wasn't in it, would I still think about this company in 6 months?" If no, it's FOMO, not conviction. Apply a mandatory 48-hour cooling period before any term sheet.

---

## 2. Social Proof Bias

**Mechanism:** Assuming something is good because respected others are involved.

**VC manifestation:** "Sequoia is leading the round." "The founder was at Stripe." "YC (Y Combinator) backed this." Using other investors' involvement as a substitute for independent analysis.

**Detection signals:** Your investment thesis includes "other smart investors are in." You spend more time discussing who else is investing than analyzing the business. You can't articulate your thesis without referencing other participants.

**Countermeasure:** Conduct independent analysis BEFORE learning who else is in the round. Ask: "Would I invest if no brand-name investor were involved?" If not, you're buying social proof, not equity.

---

## 3. Anchoring

**Mechanism:** Over-weighting the first piece of information received, which becomes the reference point for all subsequent judgments.

**VC manifestation:** The founder's requested valuation anchors all negotiation. The pitch deck's TAM number anchors market sizing. Last round's valuation anchors this round's "reasonable" price.

**Detection signals:** Your valuation opinion is suspiciously close to what the founder asked for. Your market size estimate matches the pitch deck. You're evaluating the deal relative to the last round rather than from first principles.

**Countermeasure:** Before seeing the pitch, do your own bottom-up valuation. Estimate market size independently. Only then compare to the founder's numbers. The gap between your estimate and theirs is informative.

---

## 4. Narrative Fallacy

**Mechanism:** Preferring coherent stories over messy data. A compelling narrative makes random events feel inevitable.

**VC manifestation:** Founders who tell great stories raise more easily than founders with better data. "The founder saw a gap in the market when her mother couldn't find [X]" sounds better than "we ran 47 experiments and the data pointed here." Investors construct post-hoc narratives for successful investments that obscure the role of luck.

**Detection signals:** You can retell the founder's story easily but can't recall specific metrics. The investment thesis reads like a movie plot. You're more excited by the origin story than the traction data.

**Countermeasure:** Separate narrative evaluation from data evaluation. Analyze metrics first, story second. Ask: "If this exact data came from a founder with a boring story, would I still invest?"

---

## 5. Survivorship Bias

**Mechanism:** Drawing conclusions from winners while ignoring the much larger set of losers with similar characteristics.

**VC manifestation:** "Dropbox succeeded despite a crowded market, so market competition doesn't matter." "Airbnb was rejected by every VC and still became huge, so investor consensus is meaningless." These arguments ignore the thousands of companies in crowded markets that failed and the thousands of rejected companies that died.

**Detection signals:** Your argument cites successful exceptions as evidence for a general rule. You're using outlier outcomes to justify investing in a company with outlier risk.

**Countermeasure:** For every success example, find the base rate. "What percentage of companies with these characteristics succeeded?" Demand evidence from the full distribution, not just the right tail.

---

## 6. Halo Effect

**Mechanism:** One positive attribute irrationally colors judgment of unrelated attributes.

**VC manifestation:** Stanford MBA → must be a great operator. Ex-Google → must build great products. Previous exit → must understand this market. Charismatic → must be a good manager. These connections are empirically weak.

**Detection signals:** Your founder assessment is disproportionately driven by pedigree. You describe the team as "impressive" but can't articulate specific relevant skills. Brand-name prior employers feature prominently in your thesis.

**Countermeasure:** Evaluate founder skills independently from credentials. What specific capabilities does the team have? What evidence (not credentials) supports those capabilities? Would you rate this team the same if they went to a no-name school?

---

## 7. Sunk Cost Fallacy

**Mechanism:** Continuing to invest because of what you've already invested, not because of future expected returns.

**VC manifestation:** "We've already put $2M into this company — we have to do the bridge." "We've spent 3 months on diligence — we should invest." Following on into deteriorating companies to protect prior investment rather than allocating capital to better opportunities.

**Detection signals:** Your argument for investing includes "we've already invested" or "we've put a lot of work into this." You're evaluating the follow-on based on what you've spent, not on the current risk/return profile.

**Countermeasure:** Apply the "clean sheet" test: "If we had no prior investment and saw this company for the first time today, at these terms, would we invest?" If no, don't invest — regardless of what you've already put in.

---

## 8. Confirmation Bias

**Mechanism:** Seeking, interpreting, and recalling information that confirms your existing belief.

**VC manifestation:** After initial excitement about a deal, selectively reading diligence data to confirm the positive thesis. Interpreting ambiguous metrics as positive. Asking reference checks leading questions. Spending more time researching the market opportunity than the competitive threats.

**Detection signals:** Your diligence findings uniformly support the investment. You haven't found a single disconfirming data point. Your reference calls all came from the founder's suggested list.

**Countermeasure:** Assign an agent specifically to build the bear case (the Contrarian). Conduct backchannel references the founder didn't suggest. For every positive data point, actively seek a disconfirming one.

---

## 9. Availability Heuristic

**Mechanism:** Judging probability based on how easily examples come to mind, rather than actual frequency.

**VC manifestation:** A recent spectacular exit in a sector makes all deals in that sector look better. A recent high-profile failure makes similar companies look riskier. Recent portfolio success in fintech makes you overweight your ability to pick fintech winners.

**Detection signals:** Your thesis includes "we just saw [Company X] exit at 50x." You're avoiding a sector because of a recent failure you witnessed. Your deal pipeline is clustered around whatever sector had the most recent press coverage.

**Countermeasure:** Base-rate everything. "What percentage of companies in this sector at this stage actually achieve this outcome?" Use data, not memory.

---

## 10. Overconfidence Bias

**Mechanism:** Overestimating your own predictive accuracy. Believing your analysis is more certain than it actually is.

**VC manifestation:** "I'm 90% sure this will be a 10x return." High-conviction calls made with insufficient data. Concentrating bets based on certainty that isn't warranted. Underweighting tail risks because you "understand the market."

**Detection signals:** You rarely express uncertainty in your position statements. Your confidence level doesn't change much after diligence. You can't articulate the top 3 things that would make your thesis wrong.

**Countermeasure:** Calibrate by tracking your prediction accuracy over time. Require every position statement to include explicit uncertainty ranges and named conditions for being wrong.

---

## 11. Bandwagon Effect

**Mechanism:** Adopting beliefs and behaviors because many others have done so. Distinct from social proof — it's about the *volume* of adoption, not the *quality* of adopters.

**VC manifestation:** "Everyone is investing in AI right now." "The whole market is moving to [trend]." Investing in sectors because they're hot, not because the specific deal has merit. Herding behavior in VC leads to bubble formation and compressed returns.

**Detection signals:** Your thesis references market momentum or investor enthusiasm as evidence. You're competing for deals primarily in the hottest sectors. Your portfolio is highly correlated with industry trends.

**Countermeasure:** Ask: "If I were the only investor looking at this sector, would I still invest?" Track whether your best returns came from consensus deals or contrarian ones.

---

## 12. Recency Bias

**Mechanism:** Overweighting recent events and underweighting historical patterns.

**VC manifestation:** A single strong quarter is treated as proof of trajectory. Recent market conditions are assumed to persist. The last fund's strategy is applied to this fund regardless of market changes. A recent bad experience with a founder archetype poisons evaluation of similar founders.

**Detection signals:** Your analysis focuses on the last 2-3 months of data and ignores longer trends. You're extrapolating a recent quarter linearly. Your risk assessment is calibrated to the current market environment without considering regime changes.

**Countermeasure:** Always look at the longest available data series. Ask: "What does the 24-month trend show vs. the 3-month trend?" If they diverge, investigate which one is the signal and which is the noise.
