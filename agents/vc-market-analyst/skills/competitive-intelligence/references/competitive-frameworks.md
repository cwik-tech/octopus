# Competitive Frameworks

## Porter's Five Forces (Adapted for Startup Evaluation)

### 1. Threat of New Entrants

How easy is it for new competitors to enter this market?

**Low threat (good for startup):** High capital requirements, strong network effects once established, regulatory barriers, deep technical moat, long development cycles.

**High threat (bad for startup):** Low capital requirements, off-the-shelf technology, no regulatory barriers, easily replicable product. If it's easy for this startup to enter, it's easy for the next one too.

**VC-specific question:** "If we fund this company and they succeed, what prevents 10 well-funded copycats from entering within 18 months?"

### 2. Bargaining Power of Suppliers

Does the startup depend on a small number of suppliers who could extract value?

**Critical suppliers in tech:** Cloud providers (AWS/GCP/Azure), data providers, key APIs, chip manufacturers, AI model providers (OpenAI, Anthropic).

**Red flag:** Single-source dependency. If the startup's product depends entirely on one API that could raise prices, change terms, or build a competing product, that's supplier risk.

### 3. Bargaining Power of Buyers

Can customers easily switch or negotiate prices down?

**Low buyer power (good):** Many small customers, high switching costs, mission-critical product, no alternatives.

**High buyer power (bad):** Few large customers (customer concentration), easy switching, commodity product, many alternatives. Enterprise customers with procurement departments have inherently high bargaining power.

### 4. Threat of Substitutes

Can customers solve the same problem with a completely different approach?

**Common substitutes:** Manual processes, spreadsheets, in-house development, outsourcing to services firms, offshore labor, not solving the problem at all.

**VC-specific question:** "Is the customer's alternative to buying this product really 'nothing'? Or will they just hire an intern to do it in spreadsheets?"

### 5. Competitive Rivalry

How intense is competition among existing players?

**Indicators of intense rivalry:** Price wars, rapid feature copying, aggressive sales tactics, negative-margin customer acquisition. Intense rivalry compresses margins and increases capital requirements.

**Indicators of manageable rivalry:** Differentiated positioning, segment specialization, pricing discipline, market growing fast enough for multiple winners.

## Strategy Canvas Method

Plot all competitors on a single chart where:
- X-axis: Key competitive factors (price, ease of use, feature depth, integrations, support, vertical specialization, etc.)
- Y-axis: Performance level on each factor (low to high)

**What to look for:**
- **Convergence:** All players have similar profiles → commoditization risk. The startup must either find a different set of factors to compete on or accept margin compression.
- **Divergence:** Players are differentiated on different factors → healthy market with room for specialized positioning.
- **Gaps:** Factors where no player scores high → potential whitespace for the startup.

## Competitive Response Models

### The Innovator's Response Matrix

When a startup enters a market, incumbents respond based on two factors:

| | Low threat to core business | High threat to core business |
|---|---|---|
| **Easy to replicate** | Ignore → Copy later if needed | Copy immediately with full resources |
| **Hard to replicate** | Observe → Maybe acquire | Acquire aggressively or compete with new division |

**Best position for a startup:** Hard to replicate + Low initial threat to incumbents. This buys time to build scale before incumbents react. This is the classic "disruptive innovation" path — attack a segment incumbents don't care about with a product that gets better over time.

**Worst position:** Easy to replicate + High threat. The incumbent will respond fast and has more resources.

### Response Timeline Estimation

| Moat Type | Estimated Incumbent Response Time |
|---|---|
| Feature copy (no moat) | 3-6 months |
| UX/design advantage | 6-12 months |
| Data advantage | 12-24 months |
| Network effect | 18-36 months |
| Deep technology | 24-48 months |
| Regulatory moat | 36+ months |

Multiply by 0.5x if the incumbent is a big tech company (they move faster). Multiply by 2x if the incumbent is in a regulated industry (they move slower).
