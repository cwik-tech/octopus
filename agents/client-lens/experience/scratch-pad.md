### Session: 2026-03-31 — Production readiness gap analysis for multi-agent orchestration runtime

- **What worked:** The stakeholder mapping move — surfacing unnamed stakeholders (operators, finance, technical decision-maker, consuming teams) gave the analysis a distinct lane from the other agents. The Hallway Conversation heuristic produced concrete, quotable reactions from each stakeholder type, which made the political fragility tangible rather than abstract. Naming the budget enforcement decision as a stakeholder management task (not a development task) was a precise, actionable insight no other agent produced.
- **What didn't:** The position leaned heavily on "the prerequisite question must be answered first" without fully specifying how the client should go about answering it. The closing question was sharp but the path to answering it was underspecified — a sponsor reading the output would agree with the diagnosis but not know the next action step. The analysis was slightly too long to serve as the champion-able one-page artifact it advocated for.
- **Pattern noticed:** In technical roadmap sessions, the client-lens contribution is most valuable when it reframes a technical sequencing debate into a stakeholder alignment sequence. The other agents (Contrarian, Pragmatist, Domain Researcher) naturally converge on "what to build and in what order." The political gap — who must decide, who must approve, who will block — is consistently absent from their framing and is where client-lens earns its seat.
- **For next time:** When advocating for a "prerequisite question must be answered," immediately specify the format and owner of the answer: who answers it, in what meeting, with what deliverable, by when. Abstract prerequisites stall in the hallway the same way abstract roadmaps do. Also consider producing the one-page champion-able artifact directly within the position statement, rather than describing what it should contain — show, don't prescribe.

### Session: 2026-04-04 — Michigan automotive market entry for Ubivis (Brazilian IIoT company)

- **What worked:** The three-layer stakeholder map — explicitly named, unnamed-but-controlling, and hidden-until-it's-too-late (IT/OT security gatekeepers) — gave the position a distinct lane no other agent occupied. The "principal-agent commitment problem" framing landed well: it recast what looked like a market access problem as an organizational commitment problem, which is the more accurate and more actionable diagnosis. The Hallway Conversation heuristic produced four distinct quotable reactions (US office lead, Brazilian CEO, Denso Michigan plant manager, Tier 2 plant manager) that made the political fragility visceral and memorable rather than abstract. Producing the champion-able one-page ask directly ("We need you to make two calls in the next 30 days...") rather than describing what such an artifact should contain was an improvement over the prior session — the deliverable was embedded in the analysis, not just described.
- **What didn't:** The position grew longer than necessary and repeated the HQ sponsorship point across multiple sections without tightening it into a single definitive statement. The Sponsor Test section analyzed each recommendation serially, which is useful but slightly mechanical — it would have been sharper to rank the recommendations by political survivability rather than review them in the order others introduced them. The closing governance question ("Who is the named Brazilian executive sponsor?") is the right question, but it still lacks the follow-on specification: what meeting, what format, what 30-day deliverable proves the answer is real and not rhetorical.
- **Pattern noticed:** In foreign-market-entry sessions, the client-lens uniquely owns the principal-agent diagnosis — no other agent naturally asks "who in HQ has to do something for this strategy to work, and are they willing?" The other agents (Pragmatist, Domain Researcher, Innovator, Pattern Matcher) converge on what the strategy should be and which entry vectors to pursue, but consistently assume HQ activation happens automatically once the strategy is sound. The political gap is always: the strategy requires a Brazilian CEO phone call that the US office cannot make and HQ has not been asked to make. This gap recurs across all foreign subsidiary market entry problems.
- **For next time:** When the principal-agent commitment problem is the core finding, name it once, sharply, and immediately present the minimum viable test that determines whether the principal is committed or not — in this case, "Has the Brazilian CEO been asked to make the Denso call yet, and what happened?" If the answer is unknown, that single question should be the first action item, before any strategy work continues. Also: when producing the champion-able artifact, specify not just the two calls but the exact framing for each — who calls whom, what they say, and what a successful outcome looks like. The artifact is only champion-able if the US office lead can read it aloud to the Brazilian CEO without translation.
