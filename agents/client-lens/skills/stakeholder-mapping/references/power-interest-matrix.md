# Power/Interest Matrix (Mendelow)

## Overview

The Mendelow Power/Interest Matrix is a stakeholder prioritization framework developed by Aubrey Mendelow. It maps stakeholders on two dimensions—**power** (ability to affect outcomes) and **interest** (level of concern about the engagement)—to determine engagement strategy.

## Dimensions Explained

**Power**: The stakeholder's ability to influence or block decisions, allocate resources, or shape outcomes. Assess by:
- Authority in the org (formal role, signature rights)
- Control of resources (budget, headcount, technology)
- Influence over key decision-makers (trusted advisor status)
- Ability to escalate or veto (reporting line, committee membership)

**Interest**: The stakeholder's active concern about the engagement's outcome. Assess by:
- Direct impact on their role or department
- Stated priorities or public commitments
- Resource commitment or visibility
- Mention in problem statement or charter

## The Four Quadrants

### 1. High Power, High Interest — **MANAGE CLOSELY**
- **Profile**: Decision-makers, executive sponsors, champions, primary blockers.
- **Action**: Daily or weekly alignment. Provide complete information, invite to design sessions, address concerns in real-time.
- **Risk**: Misalignment here derails the engagement. Invest heavily in understanding their incentives and keeping them aligned.
- **Example**: CIO who requested the digital transformation initiative; CFO who controls the budget and cares deeply about ROI.

### 2. High Power, Low Interest — **KEEP SATISFIED**
- **Profile**: Budget gatekeepers, compliance officers, senior leaders in adjacent areas.
- **Action**: Keep informed with brief, outcome-focused updates. Remove friction; respect their time. Flag escalations proactively.
- **Risk**: They can block or slow progress if dissatisfied, but won't volunteer engagement. Over-engage and waste their time; under-engage and lose approval.
- **Example**: Board members who must approve large capex; legal counsel who reviews contracts but isn't day-to-day involved.

### 3. Low Power, High Interest — **KEEP INFORMED**
- **Profile**: End users, process owners, departmental advocates, project team members.
- **Action**: Involve in working sessions, gather feedback, use as advocates. Communicate changes early and often.
- **Risk**: These stakeholders drive adoption. Exclude them and the solution fails in practice, even if formally approved.
- **Example**: Call center managers whose staff will use new systems; department heads affected by process changes.

### 4. Low Power, Low Interest — **MONITOR**
- **Profile**: Secondary stakeholders, passive observers, future-phase users.
- **Action**: Monitor for shifts in interest or power. Add to general communications but don't over-invest.
- **Risk**: Usually low, unless they uncover an unexpected dependency or their interest suddenly increases.
- **Example**: IT security team in phase 1 (security deep-dive comes in phase 2); sister divisions not directly affected.

## Classification Logic

**Determining Power:**
- Ask: "Can this person block the project, kill the budget, or force a restart?"
- If yes → High Power.
- Ask: "Do they control resources or approval needed to move forward?"
- If yes → High Power.

**Determining Interest:**
- Ask: "Is this person's role directly affected by the problem or solution?"
- If yes → High Interest.
- Ask: "Did they request this engagement or mention it as a priority?"
- If yes → High Interest.
- Absence of objection or visibility ≠ Low Interest. Probe to confirm.

## Worked Example: Digital Payments Platform

**Scenario**: A retail company is implementing a new digital payments platform to reduce fraud and improve customer experience.

**Stakeholder Analysis:**

| Stakeholder | Role | Power | Interest | Quadrant | Engagement |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chief Digital Officer | Executive sponsor | High | High | Manage Closely | Weekly steering, co-design phases, escalation authority |
| CFO | Budget holder | High | Medium-High | Manage Closely | Monthly ROI reviews, fraud savings reporting, fraud risk mitigation |
| VP Fraud & Risk | Risk owner | High | High | Manage Closely | Weekly security reviews, threat modeling, system sign-off |
| VP Retail Operations | Ops leader | Medium-High | High | Manage Closely | Daily point-of-sale testing, store rollout planning, adoption metrics |
| IT Director | Technical delivery | Medium | High | Keep Informed | Sprint ceremonies, infrastructure sign-off, support ramp |
| Store Managers (x50) | End users | Low | High | Keep Informed | Pilot groups, feedback loops, training, go-live support |
| Legal Counsel | Compliance | High | Medium | Keep Satisfied | Quarterly PCI/compliance updates, vendor contracts, risk waiver sign-offs |
| Board Member (Retail Committee) | Governance | High | Low | Keep Satisfied | Quarterly board updates, KPI reporting only |
| Customer Service Manager | Secondary ops | Low | Medium | Monitor | Escalation path, training for dispute handling |

**Strategic Implications**:
- The CFO and VP Fraud & Risk are equally critical; their approval gates the platform.
- Store managers drive adoption but have low formal power; invest in pilot feedback and training.
- The board member must be kept satisfied with clean metrics, but doesn't need involvement in design.
- The customer service manager monitors for escalation; their role becomes high-interest in post-launch support phase.

## Updating the Matrix

Stakeholder power and interest shift as the engagement evolves:
- Early phase: Executive sponsors dominate (high power, high interest).
- Implementation phase: Ops and IT rise in interest; sponsors may drop to high power/low interest.
- Post-launch: End users rise in interest; executive sponsors may exit.

**Review the matrix every 4-6 weeks** and adjust engagement strategy accordingly. Document what changed and why.
