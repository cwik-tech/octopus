# Audience Archetypes — Reference

Detailed profiles of the five most common audiences in business consulting documents. Use during audience analysis in Step 2 of the Brief Scoping protocol.

---

## Archetype 1 — The Decision-Maker

**Typical titles:** CEO, CFO, COO, Board Member, Managing Director, Partner

**What they want:** The answer. Immediately. They don't want to be walked through your analysis — they want your conclusion and enough reasoning to defend it if challenged.

**Reading behavior:**
- Reads the executive summary only, unless the summary raises a question they need answered
- Scans headings before reading any body text
- Will stop reading the moment the document loses their thread
- Expects to finish in under 10 minutes

**What earns their trust:**
- Confident, unhedged language ("we recommend X" not "one option might be X")
- Concrete numbers, not vague qualifiers
- Explicit trade-offs — they know every decision has a cost, so naming it builds credibility
- Short sentences. They equate length with uncertainty.

**Common mistakes:**
- Building up to the recommendation instead of leading with it
- Explaining methodology in detail — they don't care how you got there
- Presenting more than 3 options — too many options is a failure to exercise judgment
- Using passive voice — it reads as evasion

**Document length:** Under 5 pages for recommendations. 8-10 pages maximum for complex assessments.

---

## Archetype 2 — The Evaluator

**Typical titles:** VP, Director, Senior Manager, Division Head

**What they want:** Options with clear trade-offs. They need to make a recommendation upward, so they need enough detail to defend the choice they make.

**Reading behavior:**
- Reads the executive summary first, then jumps to sections relevant to their function
- Will read supporting analysis if it helps them anticipate objections
- Often shares the document with their own team — structure for scanning matters
- Tolerates 5-15 pages if it's organized clearly

**What earns their trust:**
- Clear options framing (not a foregone conclusion)
- Evidence that their functional concerns were considered
- Honest risk assessment for each option
- Data they can re-use in internal presentations

**Common mistakes:**
- Not acknowledging implementation complexity — evaluators know what execution looks like
- Presenting only one recommendation with no alternatives
- Omitting risk — they need to know what they're defending against
- Charts and graphs without interpretation — they don't have time to decode

**Document length:** 5-15 pages. Use appendices for supporting data.

---

## Archetype 3 — The Implementer

**Typical titles:** Manager, Team Lead, Project Manager, Analyst

**What they want:** Specifics. What exactly should they do, in what order, by when, with what resources? They are not making the decision — they are executing it.

**Reading behavior:**
- Reads the full document, often multiple times
- Will reference the document repeatedly during execution
- Annotates and shares with their team
- Tolerates length if it's actionable — detail is a feature

**What earns their trust:**
- Step-by-step guidance rather than principles
- Ownership and accountability clarity (who does what)
- Timelines and milestones
- Answers to the "but what about..." questions they will inevitably encounter

**Common mistakes:**
- High-level recommendations without implementation guidance
- Assuming context they don't have — be explicit about dependencies
- No section on risks/blockers — they need to know what can go wrong
- Missing owners for action items

**Document length:** As long as needed. Completeness over brevity.

---

## Archetype 4 — The Stakeholder

**Typical titles:** Head of [function], VP of [function], peer-level executive from a different department

**What they want:** To understand how this affects their area and whether their concerns were heard.

**Reading behavior:**
- Reads only the sections relevant to them
- Scans for their team's name, their budget, their processes
- Looking for unwelcome surprises — will they be asked to do something disruptive?
- Often forwards the document to their own team without reading it fully

**What earns their trust:**
- Explicit acknowledgment of cross-functional dependencies
- Sections clearly labeled so they can jump to what's relevant
- Their constraints recognized, even if not fully accommodated
- Being consulted, not just informed

**Common mistakes:**
- Burying cross-functional implications in body text
- Assuming they share the same priorities as the primary audience
- Not acknowledging the burden the recommendation places on their team
- Generic formatting — stakeholders skim, so structure must support it

**Document length:** Moderate (5-10 pages). Clear headings. Dense appendices are fine.

---

## Archetype 5 — The External Client

**Typical titles:** Any client role — may be any of the above archetypes, but they are external

**What they want:** Confidence in the consultant's expertise. Proof that the money was well spent. A clear path forward.

**Reading behavior:**
- Varies by seniority (see archetypes 1-4 for reference)
- Will judge the document's quality as a proxy for the quality of the thinking
- Often shares with colleagues who weren't in the room — the document must stand alone
- Will quote the document back to you — language precision matters

**What earns their trust:**
- Professional formatting and clean prose — presentation signals capability
- Specific knowledge of their business, industry, and context
- Honest assessment, including things they may not want to hear
- Clear next steps that respect their time and resources

**Common mistakes:**
- Generic language that could apply to any client ("companies in this space often...")
- Overuse of consulting jargon
- Recommendations that require more resources than they have
- Missing the "so what for us specifically" — insights must be client-specific, not general

**Document length:** Matches the scope of engagement. For strategy: 10-20 pages. For assessments: 15-30 pages. Always with an executive summary.
