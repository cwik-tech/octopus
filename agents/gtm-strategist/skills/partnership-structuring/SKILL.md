# Partnership Structuring

## Purpose

A partnership is not a relationship. It's a deal with clear incentives, boundaries, and economics.

This skill translates channel strategy into concrete partnership terms: margin splits, volume commitments, support promises, exclusivity clauses, and incentive structures that are realistic at year-one scale and sustainable as volume grows.

The output is a proposal you can take to a partner and negotiate from.

## Framework

### Step 1: Understand Partner Motivation

Before designing terms, understand *why* a partner would choose your product over alternatives.

**Distributor motivation:**
- Margin (higher is better, but must be realistic)
- Volume potential (can this product generate 50+ orders/year in our territory?)
- Customer stickiness (do customers reorder, or is it one-time sales?)
- Competitive position (do we have a product competitors don't?)
- Support (do we help them train and market?)
- Speed to market (can they start carrying in 2–3 months, or do we require 6+ months of onboarding?)

**Design firm / architect motivation:**
- Solves a customer problem (do their customers want this product?)
- Differentiates their offering (can they charge a premium for design services using our product?)
- Simplifies their workflow (do we reduce their design time or specification burden?)
- Revenue upside (do they make money on our product, or is it a value-add?)
- Support and training (do we help them specify and support installations?)

**Contractor / GC motivation:**
- Solves their project problems (can they install faster, cheaper, or better using our product?)
- Margin (do they make money on bundled pricing?)
- Supply chain reliability (can we deliver on time, or do project schedules slip?)
- Customer demand (do their customers ask for this, or does the GC have to sell it?)
- Liability and warranty (who bears the risk if something goes wrong?)

**Agents / reps motivation:**
- Commission (typically 15–30% of gross margin per sale)
- Territory exclusivity (do they get exclusive rights to their territory?)
- Lead generation support (do you help them find customers?)
- Customer acquisition effort (how much time does each sale take?)
- Repeat business potential (do customers reorder, or is it one deal per customer?)

**Honest answer:** If you can't articulate *why* a partner would choose you, you haven't earned the right to negotiate terms. Revisit positioning or product fit before approaching partners.

### Step 2: Model Partner Margin

What margin can you afford to give partners, and at what volume does it work?

**Distributor margin example:**

| Factor | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Selling price to customer | $1,000 per unit |
| Manufacturing cost | $300 |
| Gross margin available | $700 (70%) |
| Distributor typical margin (30–50%) | 40% = $400 per unit |
| Your net margin | $300 per unit (30%) |
| Sales cost (your effort, support) | $100 per unit |
| Profit per unit | $200 per unit (20%) |
| Year-1 distributor volume target | 50 units = $10K profit |

**Is this sustainable?** Only if:
- Distributors can actually move 50+ units in year one (not a guess; validate with distributors before committing)
- Your manufacturing cost holds at $300 (or volume improves margin)
- Distributor doesn't demand additional co-op marketing funds or inventory carries that eat into your margin

**Honest assessment:** If your product only pencils out at 200+ unit volumes, it's not a distributor product. It's a direct-sales product. Say so.

**Margin structure options:**

1. **Straight wholesale:** Distributor buys at 40% discount, sells at 100% markup (their cost). Simple. No incentive for high volume.
2. **Tiered margin:** 40% for 0–25 units, 35% for 26–50 units, 30% for 50+ units (gives distributor reason to push volume).
3. **Volume rebate:** Distributor buys at 40%. After 50 units in a year, they get a $50 rebate per unit on future orders. (Rewards loyalty and volume.)
4. **Co-op marketing:** Distributor gets 40% margin + co-op fund ($500/quarter) to fund marketing. (Shares volume risk.)

**Choice:** Pick one structure per channel. Do not offer multiple options; negotiate from one position.

### Step 3: Define Volume Commitments

What volume does a partner commit to, and what happens if they fall short?

**Year-one commitment structure:**

| Period | Minimum Order | Rationale | Penalty/Incentive |
|--------|---|---|---|
| Month 1–3 | 5 units (pilot/evaluation) | Partner gets to know product and customers. No hard commitment. | If after 3 mo they've sold 5, discuss year-end forecast. |
| Month 4–12 | 2 units/month (24 units/year target) | Baseline expectation; partner allocates one person 10% time to sales/support. | If partner hits 40+ units, volume rebate kicks in. |
| Year 2 | 50+ units/year (stretch) | Assumes partner has customer base and market education from year one. | Margin improvement for 50+ volume (see Step 2). |

**Minimum order quantities (MOQs):**
- Start conservative. For year one, MOQ of 5–10 units per order (allows distributor to test customer demand without overcommitting).
- Negotiate minimum purchase/carry (e.g., distributor carries 15 units inventory) only after they've proven they can move product.

**Volume shortfall clauses:**
- If distributor doesn't sell 12+ units in year one (1 unit/month average), you both agree to reassess partnership in Q4.
- Do NOT claw back margin or penalize honest shortfalls. A distributor that tries hard but falls short is still a valuable partner; a distributor that doesn't try won't hit volume no matter what penalty you impose.
- Honest clause: "If distributor cannot commit to at least Y units/year, they should be transparent so we can redirect resources to other channels."

### Step 4: Design Support and Service Terms

What do you commit to providing, and what does the partner commit to doing?

**Your commitments (what you provide):**
- **Product training:** Initial online training session + annual refresher (not unlimited hands-on for every customer)
- **Sales/spec support:** Partner can email or call with customer questions. 24-hour response time (not 1-hour).
- **Marketing/co-op:** Provide spec sheets, CAD drawings, reference photos. Optional co-op budget (see margin structuring).
- **Warranty/support:** You warrant product for 2 years. Partner handles first-line customer support; you handle technical issues.
- **Lead generation:** You'll introduce any customers from your direct channel that fall in the partner's territory (not guaranteed volume).

**Partner commitments (what they do):**
- **Sales effort:** Actively market to their customer base. (Not just list us in their catalog.)
- **Customer support:** First-line questions, installation support, troubleshooting.
- **Feedback loop:** Monthly or quarterly feedback on customer feedback, product requests, competitive issues.
- **Reference:** Willing to share customer references with your sales team (with customer permission).
- **Exclusivity (if applicable):** Don't carry direct competitor X for Y months. (Negotiate reasonable exclusivity or none.)

**Exclusivity caution:** Exclusive partnerships often fail because partners don't have incentive to move volume (no competitive pressure). Prefer non-exclusive with incentives (volume rebates) over exclusive at lower margin.

### Step 5: Build a Partnership Proposal

Write a 1–2 page proposal to present to the partner. Include:

**Executive Summary:**
- What problem your product solves
- Why this partner is a good fit
- What's in it for them (margin, volume potential, customer benefit)

**Proposed Terms:**
- Margin: [40% wholesale discount] or [tiered] or [commission]
- Year-1 volume target: [2–5 units/month]
- MOQ: [5 units/order]
- Support from you: [training, spec support, co-op, lead generation]
- Support from partner: [active sales effort, customer support, feedback]
- Exclusivity: [none / limited to X competitor for Y months]
- Term: [12 months, then reassess]

**Differentiation:**
- Why your product vs. alternatives
- Customer testimonials or case studies (if available)
- Spec sheets, pricing, delivery timeline

**Next Steps:**
- Partner review period (1 week)
- Discussion call to negotiate terms
- Pilot order or trial period (optional)

**Tone:** Professional, but collaborative. Invite negotiation on specific terms. Show willingness to adjust margin or commitments if the partner has a compelling reason.

### Step 6: Negotiate and Agree

**Negotiation principles:**
- Start with your proposal, not a blank slate. Make the partner negotiate from your position.
- Have 2–3 negotiable points (e.g., margin, support level, volume target) and non-negotiable points (e.g., exclusivity boundaries).
- Always tie margin to volume. Higher margin = higher volume expectation. Lower margin = lower commitment.
- Agree on how you'll measure success and reassess in 12 months. This gives both sides a graceful exit if it's not working.

**Red flags during negotiation:**
- Partner wants high margin (>50%) but can't commit to volume. This suggests they don't believe in the product or don't have the customer base. Pass.
- Partner wants exclusivity without committing to volume. Exclusivity isn't free; it's only valuable if volume is high. Demand volume commitment + margin adjustment.
- Partner wants all your co-op and support but plans to invest minimal sales effort. Push back: support is proportional to their effort.

**Agreement document:**
- Keep it simple: 1–2 pages covering margin, volume, support, exclusivity, term, and how you'll reassess in 12 months.
- Include specific success metrics (e.g., "Partner commits to 24 unit orders in year one, minimum 2 units/month, target 3+/month").
- Include reassessment clause: "In November, partners and [you] will review year-one performance. If volume targets fell short, both parties agree to discuss continuation, adjustments, or mutual wind-down."

## Common Mistakes

**Offering high margin to every potential partner.** You can't afford 50% margin to 10 different distributors. Choose 2–3 strategic partners, offer good margin, expect high volume.

**Confusing partner enthusiasm with commitment.** A partner who says "this is interesting" has not committed. Written agreement + MOQ = commitment.

**Overestimating partner effort.** Most partners won't aggressively push a product that's unfamiliar to their customers. You need marketing support and lead generation, not just margin.

**Building exclusivity into year-one partnerships.** Exclusivity only works after the partner has proven they can move volume. Year one: non-exclusive, no volume = reassess.

**Creating partnership terms that don't scale.** If you offer 50% margin to a distributor expecting 50 units/year, you'll be bleeding margin for a small partner. Scale your terms with volume.

**Forgetting to set a reassessment deadline.** Partnerships without a clear "are we working?" date become zombie relationships. Set a 12-month review point from the start.

## Questions to Resolve Before Proposing

1. **What margin can we afford at year-one volume?** (Be honest: if you can't pencil out at small volume, the channel isn't ready.)
2. **What volume does this partner actually move in their existing categories?** (Don't assume they can move your product at the same velocity.)
3. **Do we have competitors in this channel already?** (If yes, what margin are competitors offering? Match or differentiate.)
4. **What support can we realistically provide at year-one scale?** (Don't promise 24/7 if you have one person.)
5. **If this partnership fails in 12 months, can we survive?** (If the answer is no, you've over-indexed on this partner. Diversify.)

## Output

Deliver a partnership structuring proposal including:
- **Partner profiles** (type of partner, what they offer, what they need)
- **Proposed economic terms** (margin, volume commitment, MOQ)
- **Support commitments** (what you provide, what they provide)
- **Year-1 volume forecast** (conservative, realistic)
- **Reassessment plan** (how you'll measure success and decide continuation)
- **Proposal template** (1–2 pages ready to send to partner)
- **Negotiation strategy** (what's flexible, what's not)
