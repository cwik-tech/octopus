# Pilot Customer Strategy

## Purpose

Does your first customer drive momentum or become a sunk cost?

This skill turns pilot customers from "let's find someone willing to try us" into strategic reference cases. You identify early adopters who are opinion leaders in their segment, have urgent problems your product solves, and are willing to co-develop proof of concept alongside you. The output is a ranked recruitment plan and a reference-case roadmap.

## Framework

### Step 1: Define Your Pilot Customer Profile

Not all early adopters are valuable pilots. Define the attributes that make a pilot worth recruiting:

**Problem urgency:** Do they have the problem *today*? (Not "we've been thinking about this" — "we need this solved in the next 6 months.") Urgency accelerates deployment and feedback cycles.

**Segment influence:** Will this customer's experience influence others in their segment? An architect with a portfolio of high-profile projects > a solo practitioner. A facility manager at a flagship museum > a small gallery. Influence = reference value.

**Budget and decision authority:** Can they buy without a year-long committee approval? Can they allocate budget to pilots? If purchase requires board approval or committee consensus, add 6+ months to timeline.

**Willingness to co-develop:** Will they give you honest feedback? Iterate quickly? Share learnings with other architects/GCs? Or will they demand perfection on day one? Pilots who demand perfection are not pilots; they're paying customers with unrealistic expectations.

**Geographic and project fit:** Is the pilot project aligned with your product's design envelope? (E.g., if your system is optimized for 300–500 sq ft modular walls in open-concept spaces, don't pilot in a 2,000 sq ft traditional office.)

**Repeatability:** Once this customer succeeds, can you repeat that success with similar customers? If the pilot is a one-off (e.g., a custom design for a unique building), it won't generate a repeatable reference case.

**Profile template:**

- **Company/role:** [Name, title, organization type]
- **Problem:** [Specific problem they are trying to solve, timeline]
- **Influence:** [Why their voice matters in the segment, types of customers they influence]
- **Budget:** [Estimated budget, approval authority, flexibility]
- **Timeline:** [When they need a solution, flexibility]
- **Collaboration style:** [How willing to iterate, provide feedback, share learnings]
- **Project characteristics:** [Size, scope, location, design constraints]

### Step 2: Identify Candidate Pilots

For each channel you plan to pursue (e.g., architects, contractors, facility managers), identify 5–10 candidate pilots:

**Source candidates from:**
- Your existing network (warm introductions > cold outreach; add 6+ months for cold)
- Architect/designer awards and publications (architects who win awards often have influence and budget)
- Industry conferences and associations (people who speak or sponsor are often early adopters)
- Your partners or investors' networks (warm intros via trusted advisors)
- Early customer expressions of interest (if you've done outreach, who showed genuine interest?)

**Screen for fit using your profile template.** Do not recruit every interested customer; recruit the *right* customers.

### Step 3: Build a Recruitment Plan

For your top 3–5 candidate pilots, outline the outreach and recruitment sequence:

**Outreach sequence:**
1. **Warm introduction** (if possible): An investor, partner, or mutual connection introduces you. Pitch: "We have a new product that solves [problem]. [Candidate] is someone we think should see this."
2. **First conversation:** 20-minute call. Goal: Understand their problem, timeline, and appetite for pilots. Do NOT pitch features. Listen for problem urgency.
3. **Needs discovery:** If they're interested, spend 1–2 hours understanding their requirements, constraints, and success metrics. What does success look like for them?
4. **Pilot proposal:** Write a 1-pager: What problem we're solving for them, what pilot success looks like (timeline, deliverables, metrics), what we need from them (time, feedback, willingness to share learnings).
5. **Pilot agreement:** Legal/contract. Keep it simple: scope of work, timeline, confidentiality, IP ownership (you own learnings; they own their project design).

**Timeline template:**

| Candidate | Status | Warm Intro | First Call | Needs Discovery | Pilot Proposal | Agreement | Deployment Start |
|-----------|--------|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Name A] | High priority | Week 1 | Week 2 | Week 3–4 | Week 4 | Week 5–6 | Week 7 |
| [Name B] | High priority | Week 1 | Week 2 | Week 3–4 | Week 4 | Week 5–6 | Week 8 |
| [Name C] | Medium priority | Week 2 | Week 3 | Week 4–6 | Week 6 | Week 7–8 | Week 10 |

**Parallel recruitment:** Do not recruit pilots sequentially. Pursue your top 3 pilots in parallel (months 1–3) so you have 2–3 deployments starting in months 3–6.

### Step 4: Define Pilot Success Metrics

What proof do you need from each pilot to claim product-market fit and generate a reference case? Define this *before* the pilot starts, not at the end.

**Possible success metrics:**

- **Performance delivery:** Does the product meet the spec? (Use their requirements as the yardstick, not yours.)
- **Timeline:** Was deployment on schedule? (What's your contingency for delays?)
- **Cost:** Did the project stay within budget? (Did margin hold?)
- **Customer satisfaction:** Would they recommend us? (Net Promoter Score, reference-call willingness.)
- **Design precedent:** Can we use this project as a reference for similar customers? (Photos, metrics, testimonials.)
- **Repeatability:** Did we discover a repeatable deployment model? (Or did this pilot reveal design constraints that limit future projects?)
- **Learning:** What did we learn about manufacturing, installation, support, or design that improves the next pilot?

**Reference-case output:**
- Case study (1–2 pages): Problem, solution, results, testimonial)
- Reference customer (willing to take calls from prospects)
- Published images/portfolio (with permission)
- Performance data (if applicable: cost, timeline, satisfaction)

### Step 5: Prepare for Pilot Challenges

**Realistic pilot problems:**
- The pilot customer's project scope changes mid-pilot (building delays, design revisions). Agree upfront: are you following the customer or pausing the pilot?
- Installation takes longer than expected. Whose cost is the delay? (Agree upfront.)
- Customer feedback reveals product design gaps. Do you iterate in the pilot, or defer to v2? (Agree upfront.)
- Customer is not a good reference (they don't influence others, or their project was too custom). Minimize damage: move to next pilot quickly.
- Pilot timeline slips. You have no reference case by month 9. (This is why you recruit 3 pilots in parallel, not sequentially.)

**Mitigation:**
- Over-communicate with pilot customers weekly (not monthly). Catch issues early.
- Assign a dedicated person to each pilot (not split attention).
- Document learnings as you go, not at the end.
- Plan for 20% timeline slip; add contingency to your schedule.

### Step 6: Build a Pilot Roadmap

Which pilots align with which product launches?

**Example roadmap:**

| Product Version | Timeline | Pilot Customer | Scope | Success Metric | Reference Output |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| v0.5 beta | M1–M4 | [Architect A] | 300 sq ft proof of concept | On-time deployment, customer satisfaction >8/10 | Case study, reference call availability |
| v1.0 | M4–M8 | [Architect B], [GC C] | Two reference installations (different project types) | Repeatability across project types, cost/margin hold | Two case studies, portfolio images |
| v1.5 | M9–M12 | [GC D] | Large deployment (500+ sq ft) | Scale viability, installation efficiency, cost optimization | Scaled case study |

**Principle:** Each pilot should inform the next product version, and each version should enable the next cohort of pilots. Do not run pilots in isolation.

## Common Mistakes

**Recruiting every interested customer as a pilot.** Early adopters are rare; the first person who expresses interest is not always the *best* pilot. Be selective.

**Not defining success metrics upfront.** Pilots without clear success criteria become endless feedback loops. Define success before you start.

**Recruiting pilots sequentially (one finishes, next starts).** Recruit 3 in parallel so you have staggered deployments and overlapping learnings.

**Confusing beta customers with pilots.** A beta customer pays discounted price and tolerates rough edges. A pilot customer co-develops and shares learnings. They're different.

**Not generating a reference case.** A pilot that doesn't produce a case study, testimonial, or reusable design precedent is a feature request, not a pilot.

**Choosing pilots who are too different.** If your first three pilots are in completely different segments (e.g., a museum, a tech startup, a hospital), you won't learn repeatable patterns.

## Questions to Resolve Before Recruiting

1. **What does success look like for this pilot?** Be specific: on-time delivery, customer NPS >8, cost within Y%, reference willingness, design precedent.
2. **What will we do if the pilot customer's project scope changes mid-pilot?** (Agree upfront.)
3. **Are we willing to customize the product for this pilot, or is this a "take it as-is" test?** (Pilot = co-develop. Beta = take it as-is. Be clear.)
4. **How will we extract learnings so the next pilot benefits?** (Weekly notes, monthly retrospectives, dedicated owner.)
5. **What's our contingency if the first pilot slips by 2–3 months?** (You'll have no reference case on the original timeline. This is why recruiting multiple pilots matters.)

## Output

Deliver a recruitment and success plan including:
- **Pilot customer profiles** (3–5 candidates, ranked by fit)
- **Recruitment timeline** (who to approach in which month, warm intro strategy)
- **Success metrics** for each pilot (quantified where possible)
- **Reference case output** (what you'll publish from each pilot)
- **Deployment schedule** (when each pilot starts, end goal timeline)
- **Contingency** (what if first pilot slips? How do you maintain momentum?)
