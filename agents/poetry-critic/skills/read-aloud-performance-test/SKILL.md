# Read-Aloud Performance Test

Meter that scans on the page can still fail in the mouth. This test assumes the reader is not a performer. They may be emotional, holding a glass or a phone, and speaking in a room with poor acoustics.

## Inputs

- The draft poem
- The delivery mode, the reader, the venue notes if any, and the target length or duration
- The form specification, including the read-aloud rhythm notes from the form specialist

## Procedure

### 1. Breath mapping

Mark natural breath points (/) where punctuation or phrasing allows a pause. Flag any span longer than about 15 to 18 syllables with no breath point. Flag any breath that falls mid-phrase and splits meaning, such as between an adjective and its noun or across a name.

### 2. Stress check

Speak each line with ordinary conversational stress. Flag three problems:

- **Wrenched stress.** The meter forces a wrong emphasis on a word.
- **Stress collision.** Three or more consecutive stressed syllables slow the line unintentionally.
- **Stumbling runs.** Long unstressed runs make the reader rush.

Metrical correctness on paper does not override natural speech.

### 3. Mouth-traps

Flag the following:

- Consonant clusters across word boundaries ('crisp spring streets')
- Repeated sibilants that hiss through a microphone
- Near-tongue-twisters
- Unfamiliar names or words without an obvious pronunciation
- Words that are hard to say while crying (long polysyllables at emotional peaks)

### 4. Heard-ambiguity

Listeners cannot see spelling or line breaks. Flag four kinds of line:

- **Homophones** that change meaning ('mourning' and 'morning', 'aisle' and 'isle')
- **Enjambment** whose meaning depends on the visual break
- **Parenthetical asides** that vanish aloud
- **Pronoun confusion.** It is unclear who 'you' or 'she' refers to when spoken.

### 5. Applause and reaction beats

Identify where the room is likely to laugh, sigh, or react. Flag a line if a laugh would drown out the next line. Suggest a stanza break or a short beat line after likely laughs.

### 6. Ending delivery

The final line must be easy to land slowly and clearly, with a falling cadence or a clear cue, such as a raised glass at a toast. Flag endings that trail off, end on a weak unstressed syllable, or depend on a visual effect.

### 7. Running time

Estimate spoken time at about 110 to 130 words per minute for emotional or ceremonial delivery. Toasts should usually run under 1.5 minutes, and eulogy poems under 3 minutes, unless the brief says otherwise. Flag an overrun of more than 25 percent as BLOCKING and a smaller overrun as a WARNING.

### Written gifts

If the delivery mode is a written gift, run a light pass. Check steps 2, 4, and 6 only, because the recipient still hears the poem in their head.

## Severity rules

- **BLOCKING:**
  - A line a nervous reader is likely to stumble on at an emotional peak or in the ending
  - Heard-ambiguity that changes meaning in a harmful or comic way
  - A running time well over target
- **WARNING:**
  - Breathless spans
  - Wrenched stress
  - Mouth-traps elsewhere
  - Laugh-overlap risks
- **SUGGESTION:**
  - Optional rhythm smoothing

## Output

Provide a Read-Aloud Map: the poem reproduced with / for breath points, bold on at-risk stressed syllables, and ⚠ beside risky lines. Follow it with a list of issues, each with the quoted line, the problem, the severity, and the smallest fix that keeps the form specification intact. Add a note on any pronunciations the reader should rehearse.
