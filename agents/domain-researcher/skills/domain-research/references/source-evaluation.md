# Source Evaluation Reference

Rapidly assess research sources during domain acquisition. Use these criteria to triage sources and flag confidence levels.

## Primary vs. Secondary Sources

**Primary sources carry higher weight:**
- Regulatory filings (SEC filings, patent applications, government reports)
- Original research (academic papers, proprietary market reports, internal case studies)
- Participant accounts (earnings call transcripts, interviews, firsthand narratives)
- Raw data (market statistics, compliance databases, published benchmarks)

**Secondary sources are faster but risk distortion:**
- News articles (useful for identifying trends; may sensationalize)
- Industry analysis (consultants' reports; may be vendor-influenced)
- Blog posts and summaries (fast but depends entirely on author credibility)
- Aggregators (platforms like Crunchbase, G2; useful for verification but not truth)

**Decision rule:** Use secondary sources to identify topics and leads. Verify with primary sources before making claims.

## Recency Bias and Checking Dates

Domain knowledge degrades rapidly. Assign confidence based on publication date:

- **Last 1-2 years:** Current. Use freely.
- **3-5 years old:** Treat with caution. Has the domain shifted? Check if source is still authoritative.
- **Older than 5 years:** Potentially obsolete. Use only to understand historical context.

**Flag outdated sources explicitly.** E.g., "As of 2020, the market leader was X. [Check current status.]"

## Vendor Bias

Marketing materials, whitepapers, and promotional content are unreliable:

- Vendors overstate adoption, understate competition, ignore weaknesses.
- Discount vendor claims by 50% unless verified by independent sources.
- Look for third-party validation (analyst reports, customer testimonials) if using vendor material.
- Note: regulatory filings by companies (10-Ks, earnings calls) are more reliable than marketing, since false statements carry legal risk.

## Regulatory vs. Industry Sources

Both have credibility, but for different reasons:

- **Regulatory sources** (government agencies, standard-setting bodies): Highly credible on rules and enforcement. Less credible on technology or market dynamics.
- **Industry sources** (trade associations, industry groups): Credible on standards and best practices. May understate risks and regulatory burden.
- **Academic sources**: Credible on research; may lag behind current practice.

**Decision rule:** Use regulatory sources as ground truth for compliance and risk. Use industry sources for operational context. Cross-check both.

## Triangulation: Building Confidence

When you find conflicting claims, triangulate:

- **One source says X.** Low confidence. Mark as "reported by [source]" and flag uncertainty.
- **Two independent sources say X.** Medium confidence. Can cite both.
- **Three+ independent sources say X.** High confidence. Can state as fact.
- **Sources conflict on X.** Flag the conflict. Investigate why (e.g., different time periods, different definitions, bias).

**Triangulation example:** "Three industry reports claim the market is consolidating. But Company A's latest SEC filing shows new entrants. Likely explanation: consolidation among incumbents, fragmentation at edges."

## Author Credentials

Assess whether the author has credibility:

- **Works in the domain.** Highest credibility. (Venture capitalist covering tech; regulator covering finance.)
- **Studies the domain.** High credibility. (Academic researcher; analyst at reputable firm.)
- **Parachutes in from unrelated field.** Lower credibility. (Data scientist covering law; lawyer covering biotechnology.) Treat as informed outsider, not expert.
- **Unknown background.** Low credibility. Cross-check with attributed sources.

## Conflict-of-Interest Checklist

Before citing a source, ask:

- Does the author profit if a claim is believed? (e.g., vendor selling the solution)
- Does the author benefit from downplaying risks? (e.g., industry association)
- Is the author paid by a faction in the domain? (e.g., consulting firm working for one side)
- Is the author's credibility staked on being "right"? (If so, they may cherry-pick evidence.)

Conflicts don't disqualify sources—they just require verification from independent sources.

## Red Flags for Source Quality

Avoid or downweight sources with:

- Vague or overloaded claims ("This technology will revolutionize everything")
- No cited sources or data backing up claims
- Apparent ignorance of basic terminology or regulatory framework
- Contradictions with multiple established sources
- Outdated information presented as current
- Heavy use of hype language ("groundbreaking," "unprecedented," "game-changing")

## When to Flag Low Confidence

State explicitly when confidence is low:

- "Source: [vendor material]; confidence is medium due to promotional nature."
- "Multiple sources agree, but all are 5+ years old. Current status unknown."
- "This claim appears in one academic paper. Needs independent verification."
- "Sources conflict. Company claims X, but regulator says Y. Likely explanation: [...]"

Low confidence signals don't disqualify findings—they just tell other agents to treat them with appropriate skepticism.
