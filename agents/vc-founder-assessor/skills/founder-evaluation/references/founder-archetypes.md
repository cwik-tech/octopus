# Founder Archetypes

Common founder profiles seen in venture-backed startups. Each has characteristic strengths, predictable risks, and specific things to evaluate during diligence.

---

## The Technical Visionary

**Profile:** Deep technical expertise. Built the core technology. Often a PhD or senior engineer from a top company. Driven by the elegance of the solution.

**Strengths:** Can build exceptional products. Attracts strong technical talent. Deeply understands what's technically possible and impossible. Can make technology decisions that create genuine moat.

**Risks:** May build for technical elegance rather than customer need. Can struggle with go-to-market, sales, and organizational management. May resist "good enough" solutions that ship faster. May undervalue business co-founders.

**What to evaluate:** Do they have a strong business co-founder? Can they articulate the customer problem (not just the technical solution)? Have they shipped a product that real users actually use, not just one that other engineers admire? How do they make prioritization decisions — by technical interest or customer value?

---

## The Domain Expert

**Profile:** 10+ years in the industry they're disrupting. Knows the customers, the buyers, the regulations, and the pain points from the inside. Left a successful career to start this.

**Strengths:** Unmatched customer empathy. Knows exactly who to sell to and what they'll pay for. Can navigate regulatory and political complexity. Immediate credibility with customers and industry partners.

**Risks:** May be too close to the current way of doing things — can't see the radical alternative. May build for their former employer rather than the broader market. May underestimate the importance of technology. First-time founder challenges (hiring, fundraising, company building).

**What to evaluate:** Can they think beyond their own experience? Are they building for the industry broadly or for one specific customer? Do they have technical talent on the team or do they think they can outsource product development? How do they handle the transition from operator to founder?

---

## The Repeat Founder

**Profile:** Built and exited (or failed at) a previous startup. Back for another round. Knows the fundraising game, the hiring game, and the scaling playbook.

**Strengths:** Pattern recognition from previous experience. Existing investor relationships. Faster execution because they've done the early stages before. Better at hiring (know what to look for). Better at fundraising (know what investors want).

**Risks:** May apply the playbook from their last company to a different market. Previous success creates overconfidence. May be less hungry than a first-time founder. May be doing this for financial returns rather than genuine mission — which affects resilience when things get hard.

**What to evaluate:** Is this market similar enough to their previous experience that the pattern applies? Are they genuinely excited about this problem or just doing "another startup"? How has their approach evolved from last time — what did they learn? Did they retain key people from their previous company (strong signal if yes)?

---

## The Hustler / Business-First Founder

**Profile:** Strong on sales, marketing, and business development. Can sell anything to anyone. High energy, great communicator, natural networker. Often an MBA or ex-consulting/banking background.

**Strengths:** Fast customer acquisition. Great at fundraising. Can build partnerships and distribution channels quickly. Excels at storytelling and positioning.

**Risks:** Product may be shallow (optimized for sales pitch rather than user value). May scale sales before product-market fit. Technical team may be weak or undervalued. Growth may be driven by charisma rather than product quality, which doesn't scale.

**What to evaluate:** Is there a strong technical co-founder? Is the product good, or is it a pitch deck with a demo? Are customers retaining, or just buying because the founder sold them? What happens to growth if the founder stops selling personally?

---

## The Academic / Researcher

**Profile:** Coming from academia or a research lab. Has a breakthrough technology or deep domain expertise from years of research. Often has publications and patents.

**Strengths:** Genuine technical differentiation. Deep expertise that can't be easily replicated. Often has access to research talent and academic partnerships. IP portfolio.

**Risks:** May struggle with the transition from research to product. "Publish or perish" mentality conflicts with "ship or die" startup reality. May resist simplifying the technology for market needs. May undervalue business, sales, and operations. Slow decision-making.

**What to evaluate:** Can they ship a product, not just publish a paper? Do they have a business co-founder who can translate research into revenue? Are they willing to simplify their technology for the market's needs? How do they handle ambiguity and speed (academia is slow and methodical; startups are fast and messy)?

---

## The Operator

**Profile:** Experienced executive from a scaling company. VP or C-level at a company that grew from early-stage to scale. Decided to be their own boss.

**Strengths:** Knows how to build and manage organizations. Strong operational discipline. Good at hiring, process design, and scaling systems. Credible to enterprise customers.

**Risks:** May over-structure too early (processes and procedures before finding product-market fit). May be too incremental — used to optimizing within an existing framework rather than creating something new. May struggle with the ambiguity and resource constraints of early-stage. "Corporate" instincts can conflict with startup speed.

**What to evaluate:** Can they operate in ambiguity? Are they comfortable with the resource constraints of a startup (no team, no budget, no infrastructure)? Do they have the creativity to find product-market fit, or are they better suited to scaling something that already works? What drives them — building something new or running something well?

## Red Flags Across All Archetypes

These concerns apply regardless of founder type:

- **Can't articulate why they chose this problem.** Generic "big market" reasoning. No personal connection or conviction.
- **Dismisses competition.** "We have no real competitors" almost always means they haven't done the research.
- **Refuses to discuss equity or cap table.** Transparency about ownership is a baseline trust signal.
- **Team attrition.** If early employees are leaving, something is wrong.
- **Reference check divergence.** If formal references are glowing but backchannel references raise concerns, trust the backchannel.
- **Uncoachable.** Defensive in response to every question. Treats investor questions as attacks rather than collaboration.
