# Cognitive Biases in Consulting Arguments

Ten biases most likely to distort deliberation. Learn to spot them and counter them.

For the extended catalogue (19 additional biases including planning fallacy, narrative fallacy, complexity bias, and consulting-specific biases), see `archive/extended-biases.md`.

## 1. Confirmation Bias

**What it is**: Seeking, interpreting, or recalling evidence that confirms what you already believe while ignoring contradictory evidence.

**How to detect it**: An argument cites only supporting data and dismisses contrary data as "not representative" or "old." The speaker has stopped testing their position.

**How to counter it**: "I see three pieces of supporting evidence. What's the strongest evidence against this position? What would change your mind?"

---

## 2. Anchoring Bias

**What it is**: Relying too heavily on an initial piece of information (the "anchor") when making decisions, even if that information is arbitrary or irrelevant.

**How to detect it**: "We've always allocated 20% to marketing, so we should continue." Or: "The competitor is valued at $500M, so we should be worth similar." No independent reasoning for the number.

**How to counter it**: "Where does that 20% come from? What would the optimal allocation be if we started from zero?"

---

## 3. Sunk Cost Fallacy

**What it is**: Continuing to invest in something because of past investment (costs already paid), not because of future potential.

**How to detect it**: "We've invested $3M in this product; we can't abandon it now." The $3M is gone; it shouldn't influence the next decision.

**How to counter it**: "The $3M was spent. Given where we are today, is the next $1M well-spent? Evaluate the future, not the past."

---

## 4. Availability Heuristic

**What it is**: Overestimating the likelihood of things that come to mind easily (recent events, vivid examples, personal experience) and underestimating things harder to recall.

**How to detect it**: "We tried something similar three years ago and it failed." That vivid failure becomes proof it won't work now, without data on base rates.

**How to counter it**: "That's a memorable example. What's the statistical pattern? How often does this fail across companies or contexts?"

---

## 5. Groupthink

**What it is**: A desire for harmony and consensus overrides realistic appraisal of alternatives. Dissenting views are suppressed; weaknesses in the dominant view go unchallenged.

**How to detect it**: Quick agreement on a position; few genuine objections; people deferring to the loudest voice rather than the strongest argument.

**How to counter it**: Explicitly request dissenting views. "What's the case against this? Who disagrees and why?" Create psychological safety for real debate.

---

## 6. Authority Bias

**What it is**: Attributing greater accuracy to the opinions of an authority figure and undervaluing contradictory evidence from non-authorities.

**How to detect it**: "The founder said we should do this, so we will." Or: "McKinsey reported that SaaS margins should be 40%, so our 35% is wrong." No independent reasoning.

**How to counter it**: "I respect the founder's view. What's their reasoning, and what evidence would we need to either confirm or challenge it?" Separate the authority from the argument.

---

## 7. Framing Effect

**What it is**: Reacting differently to the same choice depending on how it's described (as a gain or a loss, as a risk or an opportunity).

**How to detect it**: "We'll capture 40% market share" vs. "We'll miss 60% of the market." Identical situation; different reaction.

**How to counter it**: Reframe the same argument in opposite terms. "You're saying we'll win 40%. Restated: we'll fail to win 60%. How does that change your view?"

---

## 8. Survivorship Bias

**What it is**: Focusing on the cases that "survived" a selection process and overlooking those that didn't, leading to false conclusions about success.

**How to detect it**: "Every venture that scaled fast and succeeded did so because they moved fast. Therefore, we should move fast." But how many moved fast and failed? You're only seeing the survivors.

**How to counter it**: "Of the companies that pursued this strategy, how many succeeded and how many failed? That base rate matters more than the vivid winners."

---

## 9. Dunning-Kruger Effect

**What it is**: People with low competence in a domain overestimate their knowledge, while experts recognize what they don't know.

**How to detect it**: Someone advocates strongly for a position in an area where they have minimal experience, dismissing nuance and complexity.

**How to counter it**: "This seems complex. What's the strongest objection from someone who specializes in this area? Have we consulted that perspective?"

---

## 10. Status Quo Bias

**What it is**: Preferring the current state of affairs and undervaluing alternatives, even when change is clearly beneficial.

**How to detect it**: "We've always done it this way" or "It's working well enough" despite clear evidence that alternatives are superior.

**How to counter it**: "If we were starting from scratch today, would we choose the current approach? If not, what's the real reason we're not changing?"

---

## Deliberation Practice

When an argument emerges in a board deliberation:

1. **Ask**: Which of these ten could be influencing this position?
2. **Name it gently**: "I wonder if we're anchoring on the original budget" rather than "You have anchoring bias."
3. **Examine together**: "What would the argument look like if we removed that anchor?"
4. **Test resolve**: If the position holds after removing the bias, it's likely stronger than the bias alone.
