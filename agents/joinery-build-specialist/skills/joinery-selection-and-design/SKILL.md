# Joinery Selection and Design

## Objective

Choose the simplest joint that reliably meets structural, visual, and production requirements.

## Method

1. Identify the connected parts, grain directions, expected load, leverage, and visibility.
2. Classify the joint's job: alignment, tension resistance, racking resistance, shear resistance, withdrawal resistance, or seasonal movement allowance.
3. Select a joint compatible with the builder's tools and ability to make repeatable test cuts.
4. Size the joint in proportion to the parts; preserve enough long-grain material around mortises, grooves, and fasteners to prevent splitting.
5. Specify reference faces, machining order, fit target, and dry-fit acceptance criteria.
6. Offer a simpler fallback when the preferred method requires unavailable tools or excessive precision.

## Selection Guide

| Condition | Suitable approaches |
|---|---|
| Table aprons to legs | Mortise-and-tenon, loose tenon, or robust dowel joinery |
| Cabinet carcass | Dados, rabbets, sliding dovetails where movement permits, confirmat screws for sheet goods |
| Drawer corners | Half-blind or through dovetails, locking rabbets, or reinforced rabbet joints |
| Face frames | Mortise-and-tenon, loose tenons, dowels, or pocket screws where appropriate |
| Breadboard ends | Stub tenons with elongated outer-pin holes or mechanical fasteners that permit movement |
| Chair parts | Deep long-grain mortise-and-tenon or round-tenon joints sized for dynamic racking loads |

## Fit and Detail Rules

- A joint should seat fully under hand pressure or light clamping, not require force that risks splitting or distorting the assembly.
- Glue long-grain-to-long-grain surfaces; do not rely on end-grain glue alone for strength.
- Use mechanical reinforcement only when it complements the load path and does not create movement restraint.
- Make test joints from offcuts before machining final parts.

## Output

State the selected joint, its function, key proportions, cutting sequence, fit test, and fallback option.
