# Wood Movement and Structural Design

## Objective

Prevent splits, distortion, loose joints, and sag by designing around wood behavior and realistic service loads.

## Method

1. Separate solid wood from plywood, MDF, veneer-core panels, and metal components.
2. Mark grain direction for every solid-wood component.
3. Locate cross-grain restraints: screws, glue blocks, battens, stretchers, breadboard ends, frames, and captured panels.
4. Provide movement allowance through slots, buttons, figure-eight fasteners, floating panels, or sliding connections as appropriate.
5. Map static and dynamic loads, especially seating, racking, shelf contents, drawer pulls, and tabletop leverage.
6. Increase section thickness, add support, reduce span, or change grain orientation before relying on hidden reinforcement.

## Core Rules

- Solid wood moves most across grain, not along its length.
- Glue panels edge to edge with aligned grain; do not glue a wide solid panel rigidly across a frame or cross-grain support.
- Use elongated holes or purpose-made fasteners when attaching a solid top to an apron or base.
- Float frame-and-panel components so the panel can expand and contract.
- Treat chairs, stools, and benches as dynamic racking structures; prioritize long-grain engagement and triangulation.
- Treat shelves as bending members; consider span, depth, thickness, load distribution, and front-edge stiffening.

## Output

Report grain map, movement risks, load path, required allowances, and the design changes needed before construction.
