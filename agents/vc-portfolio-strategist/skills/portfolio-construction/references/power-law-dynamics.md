# Power Law Dynamics in Venture Capital

## Purpose

Understanding how the power law governs venture returns, fund returner math, portfolio construction implications, and optimal follow-on strategy.

---

## The Power Law in Venture

### Core Principle

Venture capital returns are not normally distributed. They follow a power law distribution where a small number of investments generate the vast majority of returns. The top 1-2 investments in a fund typically return more than all other investments combined.

### Empirical Data

| Return Profile | Typical Distribution |
|---|---|
| Investments returning 0-1x (losers) | 50-60% of portfolio |
| Investments returning 1-3x (modest returns) | 20-25% of portfolio |
| Investments returning 3-10x (good returns) | 10-15% of portfolio |
| Investments returning 10-50x (great returns) | 3-7% of portfolio |
| Investments returning >50x (outliers) | 1-3% of portfolio |

**Key insight:** In a 25-company portfolio, 1-3 companies will generate 80-90% of total returns.

## Fund Returner Math

### Definition

A "fund returner" is a single investment whose returns equal or exceed the total fund size. In a $100M fund, a fund returner generates $100M+ in proceeds from a single investment.

### Calculation

**Fund returner threshold:**

Fund return required = Fund size / Ownership at exit

**Example:**
- Fund size: $100M
- Initial check: $3M for 15% ownership
- Expected dilution through future rounds: 50%
- Ownership at exit: 7.5%
- Required exit valuation for 1x fund return: $100M / 0.075 = $1.33B

**This means:** For a $3M check to return a $100M fund, the company must exit at $1.33B+. For a 3x fund return (the target for a good fund), the exit must be ~$4B.

### Fund Returner Sensitivity Table

| Check Size | Entry Ownership | Exit Ownership (post-dilution) | Exit for 1x Fund Return ($100M fund) | Exit for 3x Fund Return |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $1M | 10% | 5% | $2.0B | $6.0B |
| $3M | 15% | 7.5% | $1.3B | $4.0B |
| $5M | 20% | 10% | $1.0B | $3.0B |
| $10M | 25% | 12.5% | $800M | $2.4B |

## Portfolio Construction Implications

### Portfolio Size Trade-off

**Smaller portfolio (15-20 companies):**
- Higher ownership per company
- More concentrated risk
- Each company must have higher individual return potential
- Higher variance in fund outcomes
- Better for experienced investors with strong conviction

**Larger portfolio (25-40 companies):**
- Lower ownership per company
- Diversified risk — higher probability of at least one big winner
- Each company can have lower individual return potential (more "shots on goal")
- Lower variance in fund outcomes
- Better for newer funds or broader thesis

**The math:** If each company has a 5% chance of being a fund returner, a 20-company portfolio has a 64% chance of having at least one fund returner, while a 40-company portfolio has an 87% chance.

### Concentration vs. Diversification

| Strategy | Pros | Cons | Works When |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Concentrated (15-20 deals)** | Higher ownership, bigger returns when right | Fund zero risk if no winners | Strong deal selection ability, deep sector knowledge |
| **Diversified (30-40 deals)** | More shots on goal, statistical safety | Lower ownership, muted returns | Broader thesis, less certainty about winners |
| **Barbell (10 core + 20 exploratory)** | High conviction in core + optionality | Complex to manage | Strong conviction in some deals, exploratory access in others |

### Follow-On Strategy

**The power law demands doubling down on winners.** The biggest mistake in venture is under-investing in the companies that are working.

**Optimal follow-on framework:**

| Company Performance | Follow-On Strategy | Reserve Allocation |
|---|---|---|
| Clear top-quartile (metrics accelerating, category leader) | Maximize pro-rata, consider super pro-rata | Deploy full reserve + consider buying secondary |
| Strong performer (growing, unit economics working) | Full pro-rata participation | Deploy standard reserve |
| Mixed signals (some metrics good, some concerning) | Selective participation, evaluate closely | Deploy partial reserve |
| Underperforming (declining metrics, team issues) | No follow-on unless clear turnaround plan | Reallocate reserve to winners |

## Anti-Portfolio Lessons

The anti-portfolio (companies you passed on that became enormous) teaches as much as the portfolio itself.

### Common Anti-Portfolio Patterns

| Pattern | Why Investors Pass | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| "Too expensive" | Valuation seemed high relative to current metrics | In power law, the price of a winner matters less than not having it |
| "Market too small" | TAM seemed limited | Markets can expand dramatically if the product creates new demand |
| "Too early" | The technology wasn't ready yet | Being early can look like being wrong |
| "Weird founder" | Founder didn't fit the pattern of "successful founder" | Outlier founders build outlier companies |
| "Too competitive" | Market had many competitors | Winner-take-most markets reward the best, punish the rest |

### The Regret Minimization Framework

When evaluating a deal, ask two questions:
1. "If I invest and it fails, how bad is the loss?" (Answer: 1x the check — limited downside)
2. "If I don't invest and it becomes a $10B company, how bad is the regret?" (Answer: potentially career-defining miss)

The asymmetry between bounded downside and unbounded upside means the expected cost of false negatives (passing on winners) typically exceeds the cost of false positives (investing in losers).

## Portfolio Monitoring Through a Power Law Lens

### Quarterly Assessment Questions

1. **Which companies are the emerging fund returner candidates?** (Should identify 2-3)
2. **Are we supporting winners adequately?** (Pro-rata, board involvement, value-add)
3. **Are we spending too much time on losers?** (Triage failing companies faster)
4. **What's our current portfolio power-law distribution?** (Compare to expectations)

### The Triage Framework

| Category | Companies | Time Allocation | Follow-On |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Stars** (fund returner potential) | Top 15-20% of portfolio | 40% of partner time | Maximum |
| **Solid** (3-5x return potential) | Middle 30-40% | 30% of partner time | Pro-rata |
| **Uncertain** (unclear trajectory) | 20-30% | 20% of partner time | Evaluate |
| **Impaired** (likely loss) | Bottom 15-20% | 10% of partner time | None |
