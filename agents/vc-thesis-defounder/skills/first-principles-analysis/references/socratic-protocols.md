# Socratic Protocols for Investment Deliberation

## Purpose

Questioning techniques and regress chain methodologies for auditing the foundational assumptions in investment theses. Adapted for venture capital deliberation where jargon, social proof, and narrative fallacy create blind spots.

---

## The Socratic Method — VC Adaptation

### Core Principle

The Socratic method does not assert — it questions. In an investment committee, this means the Thesis Defounder does not claim the thesis is wrong. Instead, it asks questions that force the committee to demonstrate the thesis is right with evidence, not assertion.

### Five Question Types

**1. Clarification questions** — Force precision on vague claims.
- "What do you mean by 'strong product-market fit'? By what metric?"
- "When you say 'moat,' what specific mechanism prevents a competitor from replicating this?"
- "You said the market is 'large.' What's the number, and where does it come from?"

**2. Probing assumptions** — Surface unstated beliefs.
- "What would have to be true for this growth rate to continue?"
- "You're assuming enterprise adoption will follow. What evidence supports that beyond the pitch deck?"
- "Are we assuming the regulatory environment stays the same? What if it doesn't?"

**3. Probing evidence** — Test the quality of supporting data.
- "How do we know this retention number is real? Is it from their analytics or our verification?"
- "That comparable — is it truly comparable? What's different about the market, timing, or team?"
- "The reference check was positive. But who provided the reference — the founder? Or did we find them independently?"

**4. Questioning viewpoints** — Introduce alternative perspectives.
- "What would the bear case investor say about this deal?"
- "If Sequoia passed on this, what reason might they have had?"
- "What would this company's biggest customer say is the product's weakness?"

**5. Probing consequences** — Trace implications of the thesis.
- "If this company grows at the projected rate, what does that imply about market share? Is that realistic?"
- "If we invest and the thesis is wrong, what's the most likely way we lose money?"
- "If the company achieves everything in the plan, what's the exit look like? Is that enough?"

## The Regress Chain Method

### How It Works

Take a central claim and ask "why" (or "how do we know?") repeatedly until you reach either bedrock (an observable, testable foundation) or quicksand (a circular argument or unsupported assumption).

### Protocol

1. **State the claim:** Write down the committee's central claim in one sentence.
2. **First "why":** Why is this claim true? What's the supporting evidence?
3. **Second "why":** Why does that evidence support the claim? Is the link causal or correlational?
4. **Third "why":** What's the foundation of that link? Is it testable? Has it been tested?
5. **Classify the terminus:**
   - **Bedrock:** An observable fact (data point), a tested mechanism (scientific principle), or a verified behavior (customer evidence).
   - **Quicksand:** A circular argument (the claim supports itself), an appeal to authority ("smart people believe this"), an analogy treated as proof ("it worked for Uber"), or an unsupported assertion ("everyone knows this market is growing").

### Worked Example

**Claim:** "This company has strong product-market fit."

| Level | Question | Answer | Classification |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Why do we believe they have PMF? | "Because retention is 92% at 6 months" | Evidence (if verified) |
| 2 | How do we know that retention number is accurate? | "The founder shared their Mixpanel dashboard" | Self-reported but somewhat credible |
| 3 | Is 92% retention good for this category? | "Yes, benchmark for B2B SaaS is 85%" | Bedrock — testable against industry data |
| 4 | But is retention sufficient for PMF, or do we also need expansion? | "NRR is only 98%" | Bedrock — reveals PMF may be shallow (customers stay but don't expand) |

**Terminus:** Retention chain hits bedrock (verifiable data + benchmarks), but reveals a nuance — PMF without expansion may not support the growth thesis.

## Common VC Reasoning Traps

### The Narrative Fallacy

**Trap:** A compelling founder story becomes confused with evidence of business quality. "She quit Google to solve this problem" sounds great but tells you nothing about the market or the product.

**Socratic response:** "The founder's story is compelling. Setting that aside — what does the *data* tell us about this business?"

### The Social Proof Loop

**Trap:** "If [Top VC] is investing, it must be good." But the other VC may be investing because they heard *we* were investing.

**Socratic response:** "Has [Top VC] done their own diligence, or are they relying on ours? What specific thesis do they hold?"

### The Analogy Trap

**Trap:** "This is Uber for X" — using a successful company's trajectory as evidence for an unrelated company.

**Socratic response:** "In what specific structural ways is this like Uber? What about the ways it's not like Uber?"

### The Survivorship Bias Trap

**Trap:** "Dropbox started with a simple MVP and became a $10B company. This simple MVP could too."

**Socratic response:** "How many simple MVPs failed for every one that became Dropbox? What specifically distinguishes this one?"

### The TAM Inflation Trap

**Trap:** "The healthcare market is $4 trillion. Even 0.1% is $4 billion." But 0.1% of the healthcare market is not a single company's market.

**Socratic response:** "Walk me through the bottom-up market sizing. How many specific customers, at what specific price point, equals what specific revenue?"

## The "Question Nobody Answered" Protocol

At the end of every deliberation, identify the one question that, if answered honestly, might change everything. This is usually the question that got sidestepped, answered with jargon, or met with "we'll figure that out later."

### Common "Questions Nobody Answered"

- "Why hasn't this problem been solved before? What's different now?"
- "If the product disappeared tomorrow, what would customers do?"
- "What is the company's actual competitive advantage, without using the word 'moat'?"
- "Can this company survive if it never raises another dollar?"
- "Why are we the right investor for this company?"
- "If we're wrong about this investment, what will we have been wrong about?"

### How to Surface It

Review the committee's discussion and identify:
1. Claims that were asserted but not questioned
2. Questions that were asked but deflected or answered vaguely
3. Topics that were conspicuously avoided
4. The assumption that, if wrong, invalidates the entire thesis

That assumption is your "question nobody answered."
